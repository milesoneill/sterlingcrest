**Quick answer:** A contestability period is an initial period when a life insurer may review facts on an application after a claim. It is often two years, but the contract and state rules control the details. This differs from a waiting period that limits an early death benefit. A death during the review period does not, by itself, mean the claim will be denied.

## The review looks at facts given when applying

An insurer uses the application to decide whether to issue a life policy and to set the terms. A claim during the contestability period may prompt a closer look at answers about health history, tobacco use, or other facts the form requested.

The [NAIC's life insurance definitions](https://www.naic.org/lg-file-gt-100mb/mcas_training/mcas-data-elements-2023/Life_and_Annuity.pdf) describe this as the period before an incontestability clause takes effect. That clause limits the insurer's right to challenge the contract after the stated time, subject to its terms and law. This does not mean a new policy has no value, but you need to find the actual clause and the date it starts.

## Use the dates and terms in the actual contract

You will often hear this called a two-year period, but that is not a deadline to apply to all plans. The start date, terms, benefit, and state law matter. A general guide cannot decide those details for an individual claim.

[New York Life's guide](https://www.nylaarp.newyorklife.com/Learning-Center/life-insurance-basics/2-Year-Contestability-Period-For-Life-Insurance) explains the common two-year structure. It also describes review of records from when a person applied. When you make a note for your family, use the date and terms in the contract. A date from a sales call may be different, as may the date of the first bank draft. Do not use those as a substitute for the actual clause.

## A review does not settle the claim’s outcome

An early death may lead to questions or requests for records that take more time to process. That does not prove an answer on the form was wrong. A difference in records also does not, by itself, settle what action the insurer can take. The facts, contract, and law all matter.

The [New York regulator's guidance on these claims](https://www.dfs.ny.gov/industry_guidance/circular_letters/cl2017_01) addresses improper claim practices under New York law. Those rules are specific to that state, rather than national deadlines. For a family, there is a key difference between a pending review and a written claim decision. Ask the claims team which stage the claim has reached and what issue remains unresolved.

## A waiting period addresses the benefit amount

A graded or modified benefit clause may limit what a plan pays for certain early deaths during a stated time. A contestability clause concerns review of the facts given when applying. The same policy may have both clauses, so read each one on its own.

In a hypothetical example, a plan is described as having immediate level benefits for covered deaths. That phrase does not prove it has no review clause. In the other direction, a review clause does not tell you whether an early death benefit is limited. “Two-year wait” can blur these questions. Ask for the benefit terms and the review terms by name.

## Keep other rules on separate lines

A suicide exclusion, a premium grace period, and an early benefit schedule have different roles. The review clause does not explain all of them. Its end date is also not a promise that all later claims will be paid, since the policy must remain in force. Other terms may still apply.

Keep a short policy summary with a line for each topic, beginning with the benefit schedule. Add the review clause, exclusions, and premium duties. Mark dates from the actual contract. This helps the family work out which question it is asking. It also avoids a false choice between “nothing pays for two years” and “everything pays after two years.” Neither phrase is a full account of a contract.

## Read the questions carefully when applying

Answer the question that is actually asked, checking its time frame and wording. Do not guess what an insurer probably wants to know. If you cannot recall a drug name or diagnosis, point out the gap and ask how to clarify the answer. Do not let someone use a convenient answer just to finish faster.

Keep a copy of the completed form and any later correction, then review the issued policy when it arrives. Read any attached application as well. If you find an error, use a verified company contact to ask how to address it. Keep the reply. These steps make a record of what happened. They cannot promise that a future claim will avoid review.

## For an early claim, ask about its actual status

If you are named for the benefit, start the claim rather than assuming an early death makes filing pointless. The insurer can state what records and consents it needs. Note what it requests, what you send, and the dates. Ask whether it is waiting for records, reviewing the claim, or has made a decision.

A later change to coverage can raise its own questions about how terms apply to replaced, increased, or reinstated insurance. The age of an old contract may not answer them. For an unclear or disputed decision, your state insurance department can explain its complaint process. An individual legal dispute needs advice based on the actual contract, supporting records, and governing state law.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does this review period mean there is no coverage?**

No. It concerns a possible review of facts given when applying. The amount due for an early covered death is a separate question. Read the benefit clauses and other terms to answer it.

**Is an early claim always reviewed or denied?**

Do not assume either result. An insurer may request records or examine the form. A review and a denial are different events. Ask for the actual claim status and any written decision.

**After two years, can I ignore the other terms?**

No. The contract must still be in force, and other terms may remain relevant. Use its actual clause and state rules. A common time span is not a universal payment promise.
