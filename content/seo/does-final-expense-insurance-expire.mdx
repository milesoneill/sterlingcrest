**Quick answer:** If your final expense coverage is whole life, it is designed to last for life when you meet its terms and pay the required premiums. Term life used for final costs lasts for a stated period and can reach an end date. The policy type is the first thing to check. Missed payments, surrender, or loans can also affect whether coverage stays active.

## The purpose of the policy is different from its duration

“Final expense” and “burial insurance” describe why you bought coverage: to leave your family help with costs after your death. They do not, by themselves, tell you how long the contract lasts. For that answer, look for the actual policy type on its first pages.

Whole life is common for this purpose. You pay a premium, the cost of keeping coverage active, and the insurer pays a death benefit—the money due under the contract after a covered death. Your beneficiary is the person you name to receive that money. The [California Department of Insurance's life guide](https://www.insurance.ca.gov/01-consumers/105-type/95-guides/07-life/life-ins-guide.cfm) describes whole life as designed for lifetime coverage, while term life covers a stated period.

That difference matters if your goal is to help whenever your death occurs, rather than only during the next ten or twenty years. A low payment or the phrase “final expense” on a letter cannot answer the duration question. The contract's coverage and payment schedules can.

## What keeps a whole life policy in force

A whole life policy does not usually end simply because you reach an older birthday. But lifetime coverage is not permission to stop paying whenever you choose. Check how long premiums are due and what the contract requires to keep the policy active.

Some contracts require payments for a set number of years; others require them for a much longer period. A policy described as “paid up” no longer requires regular premiums under its terms. That is a contract status; it does not mean you have added past payments together and reached the amount of the death benefit.

A fixed premium is also different from a finished payment obligation. It means the amount is set under the policy's terms, not that the insurer will keep coverage active without receiving it. Our [fixed premium guide](/resources/fixed-life-insurance-premium) explains that point. If an advertisement mentions an age limit such as 80 or 85, check whether it is a limit for new applicants. That may have nothing to do with when your existing coverage ends.

## What happens when term coverage reaches its end

Term life is meant to cover a defined period. The [NAIC's consumer life insurance overview](https://content.naic.org/consumer/life-insurance.htm) explains that a term benefit is payable if the insured person dies during the covered period. If you survive beyond it, the next step depends on the contract.

Some policies allow renewal, possibly at a higher premium and subject to an age limit. Others offer conversion, a right to change to another type of coverage under stated rules. Those options can have deadlines. You should not assume they exist, or will remain available indefinitely, just because the policy is called term life.

**Hypothetical example:** You buy a 20-year term policy at age 70 to help with final costs. Its initial period reaches age 90, but your family's potential need for that help does not necessarily end then. Before relying on renewal or a later purchase, understand the written options, possible cost changes, and the risk that new coverage may not be obtainable. This example illustrates timing, not a product recommendation.

## A missed payment can cause a lapse rather than an expiration

A lapse means coverage ends because the conditions for keeping it active have not been met, often because a required premium was not paid. That is different from a term policy reaching its planned endpoint. A returned bank draft or a bank account change can create a payment problem even when you intended to keep the insurance.

If you receive an overdue notice, contact the insurer promptly. Ask whether coverage is active, how much is due, and the last date to resolve it. A grace period may provide time to pay, but the contract and applicable rules determine its length and effect. Do not assume every policy allows the same deadline or handles a death during that period in the same way.

If the policy has already lapsed, ask about reinstatement, the process for restoring it under its rules. It may require payment and evidence about health. Sending money alone does not confirm that coverage has been restored; get the insurer's confirmation of its status.

## Loans and surrender can change what remains for your family

Whole life can build cash value, money that accumulates within the policy over time and is separate from its death benefit. Accessing it is not the same as taking money from an ordinary savings account. A policy loan usually has interest, and an unpaid balance can reduce what your beneficiary receives. Depending on the terms and balance, it can also put coverage at risk.

The [Texas Department of Insurance's life guide](https://www.tdi.texas.gov/pubs/consumer/cb018.html) explains how cash value and policy loans affect coverage. If you have an old loan, ask for a current statement showing the balance and what happens if you make no further payments toward it. Do not assume the loan has disappeared because you have not borrowed again.

Surrender means giving up the policy under its terms. A payment you receive on surrender is different from retaining a death benefit for your family. Before making that choice, ask for current values and options in writing. Our [cash value guide](/resources/burial-insurance-cash-value) explains the basic distinction.

## The dates and details worth reviewing now

A policy may list a maturity age or date. Rather than guessing that it is an ordinary expiration date, ask what your contract says happens then: continued coverage, a payment, or another provision. A statement about a different product's maturity age will not answer that question for yours.

An initial waiting period is a separate matter again. It limits what can be paid early in some contracts; it does not mean the policy ends when the period is over. Our [waiting period guide](/resources/final-expense-insurance-waiting-period) explains that feature.

A useful yearly review is simple: confirm the policy is active, premiums are current, the beneficiary is correct, and no important term or payment date is approaching. Keep the insurer's details where your family can find them. If you are considering replacement, compare the contracts and effective dates before cancelling existing coverage. The goal is to know what will still be there for your family, not just to feel reassured by the word “permanent.”

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does burial insurance end when I turn 80 or 85?**

An upper age for new applicants is different from an end date for a policy you already own. Whole life can continue beyond its application-age limit when required payments and other terms are met. Check the actual contract rather than an advertisement aimed at new buyers.

**Can I stop paying after my premiums add up to the benefit?**

Do not assume so. The payment schedule determines when a policy is paid up. The total you have paid is not, by itself, a reason to stop. Ask the insurer to confirm the remaining obligation before cancelling a payment.

**How can my family check whether a policy is still active?**

Keep the policy number and insurer contact information with your records, and let a trusted person know where they are. The insurer can explain what information it needs to confirm status or begin a claim, the request for payment after a death. An old policy document alone may not show current status.
