**Quick answer:** A life insurer will often need proof of death along with its claim forms. Before you order extra copies, ask what kind of death certificate the company needs. Check how to send it too. Official copies come from the right state or local records office. The rules can differ by place and insurer.

## Start with the insurer’s claim instructions

When a family member dies, several people may ask for a death certificate. It can be hard to know which requests need an official copy and which can use something else. Begin with the insurer's own instructions for the particular policy.

The [consumer life insurance guide](https://www.tdi.texas.gov/pubs/consumer/cb018.html) discusses proof of death and checking who is named to receive the benefit. For your own claim, ask the company what proof it needs and how to submit it.

Ask the claim team what it needs and where to send it. Check whether each person making a claim needs their own form. Confirm whether a certified copy is required and whether the company accepts the form of document you have. Do not assume a phone photo, a photocopy, and an official record are interchangeable.

You can begin asking questions before every document is ready. Explain what you have and what is still being obtained. A clear list from the company can prevent repeated orders or a package sent to the wrong place during a hard week.

## Request the record from the place where the death occurred

The starting point for an official death certificate is the state or area where the death occurred. This may be different from the person's home state or the place where a funeral will be held.

The [CDC's Where to Write for Vital Records directory](https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/w2w/index.htm) links to the appropriate offices. It explains that the federal government does not distribute copies of individual vital records. Use the linked state or area instructions for who may request a record, the information required, and any fees.

For example, if someone lived in one state but died while visiting another, do not assume the home-state office holds the record. Locate the office for the place of death first. A funeral director may also help explain the local process and what has already been ordered.

Rules for access, identification, and ordering differ. Use the official office's current instructions rather than an estimate from an old article. If someone else is helping, check who may request and receive the record. Do not assume every relative has that right.

## Order around actual needs rather than a universal copy count

There is no useful single number of death certificates that fits every family. The number depends on who needs a copy and what each accepts. Insurance, banking, and other matters may have separate requirements.

Before placing a large order, list the companies you need to contact and ask what each requires. Note whether an official copy must be sent, whether it will be returned, and whether a copy or another format is acceptable. Those answers help you decide what to order now and what may be needed later.

Budget for the copies you actually need. The [CDC’s application guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/w2w/guidelines.htm) directs families to the state or local office for the place of death and says fees can change. Check that office’s current fee and ordering options before paying. Compare the full order total, including any delivery or processing charge shown, rather than assuming a price from another state applies to you.

Keep the official record separate from everyday working notes. It contains personal information. Share it through the insurer's official claim channel, rather than giving one to every family member just in case.

Our guide to [organizing final expense insurance records](/resources/organize-final-expense-insurance-records) can help keep the insurer's name, policy details, and document location together. The purpose is to make the next step easier to find, without giving every helper access to every private record.

## A death certificate is one part of a claim

Proof of death is one step. Sending it does not by itself settle the full amount payable or complete the claim. The insurer still needs the forms and facts for that contract and claim.

Check the spelling of names and policy details on the claim forms. If a name on a certificate differs from the policy record, explain the difference and ask what other proof is needed. Do not alter an official record yourself to make it match a form.

If you believe the record contains an error, ask the issuing office how corrections work. Tell the insurer what is being addressed so it can explain its own next steps. Get the company's answer about the proof needed for that claim. A general article cannot settle it for you.

Keep a simple claim record: the insurer, policy or claim reference, document requested, date sent, and receipt status. For example, ‘certified copy sent Monday; claim team confirmed receipt Wednesday; beneficiary form still needed’ shows the next action clearly. It is more useful than marking the whole claim ‘submitted’ when one required item remains open.

## Keep the claim and the funeral payment arrangement separate

A funeral provider may need a payment decision before the insurance claim is complete. Discuss that provider's actual terms directly. Do not treat an expected insurance benefit as money already in a bank account, or assume a funeral home automatically handles every claim.

If the provider offers to help with paperwork, clarify what it will do and what remains your responsibility. A family member may still need to contact the insurer, supply information, or follow up on a missing item. Keep a copy of any arrangement you sign and have unclear terms explained.

If you have not found the policy, start with the insurer name on old mail, account records, or an agent's contact details. Our guide to [finding missing policy records](/resources/missing-life-insurance-policy-records) explains ways to organize that search. Locating a contract and making a claim are related tasks, but one does not replace the other.

Once the immediate claim work is under control, a living family member may want to make their own funeral records easier to find. That can be a separate call when you are ready. A new policy cannot pay for a death that has already occurred.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Do I need a certified death certificate for every insurer?**

Ask each insurer what it requires for the claim in question. An official copy, a photocopy, and a scanned image may be treated differently. Confirm the accepted format and submission method before ordering or sending extra records.

**Can I request a death certificate from the CDC?**

The CDC provides a directory of state and area vital records offices. It does not distribute copies of individual death certificates. Start with the office for the place where the death occurred and follow its current instructions.

**Does sending the certificate mean the claim is approved?**

No. The document is part of the information the insurer reviews. Ask what else is needed, confirm that the submission was received, and use the company's claim status response to understand what remains open.
