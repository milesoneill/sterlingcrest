**Quick answer:** If you buy a house with someone you aren’t married to, check who signed the mortgage note, who owns the home under the deed and who would receive any life insurance benefit. Plan around what each person could afford on their own and make sure the property and insurance arrangements support the same plan.

## Start with the note, deed and beneficiary form

When two people buy a house together, the arrangement can involve three different documents. The mortgage note records who agrees to repay the loan. The deed shows who owns the home and how title is held. The beneficiary form tells the insurer who should receive a life insurance benefit if a claim is payable.

| Document | Question it answers |
| --- | --- |
| Promissory note | Who agreed to repay the home loan? |
| Deed or title documents | Who owns the home, and how would ownership transfer if one owner dies? |
| Life insurance beneficiary designation | Who would receive the benefit, and who would be next if the primary beneficiary can’t receive it? |

The [CFPB’s guide to buying with someone else](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/archive/blog/making-move-homeownership-your-own-or-someone-else/) explains that co-borrowers are jointly responsible for the mortgage. If one co-borrower stops contributing, a private agreement to split the bills doesn’t change what the lender can require under the note. Ask a local real estate or estate attorney how the deed and any co-ownership agreement work in your state. A life insurance form doesn’t change title to the property.

## Use the amount each person could pay alone

Start with the full mortgage payment, including tax and insurance amounts you pay through escrow. Escrow is money your loan servicer collects and uses to pay bills such as property taxes and homeowners insurance. Add dues, utilities or other housing expenses that are billed separately. Work out what each person could carry alone without assuming a raise or a quick sale.

Consider a hypothetical pair of co-buyers who split a $2,400 mortgage payment. If one dies, the other can afford $1,650 a month from their own income. That leaves a $750 shortfall, or $9,000 over 12 months before other household costs. If the incomes were reversed and the other person could afford $2,050, that person’s shortfall would be $350 a month, or $4,200 over the same period. Equal contributions today can lead to different needs after a death.

Next, discuss what happens if the survivor wants to keep the home for years. Ask whether they could continue making payments with help from savings or whether the plan depends on paying off part or all of the loan. Our [mortgage protection coverage guide](/resources/how-much-mortgage-protection-insurance-do-i-need) explains how to compare those goals.

## Check the insurance choices together

Mortgage protection life insurance is life insurance purchased to help with housing expenses after a covered death. If you each buy individual term life insurance, each policy covers the life of the person named for a set period. Ask the insurer who can own each policy and how to name a beneficiary under its rules. A primary beneficiary is first in line under the designation. A contingent beneficiary is the backup if the primary cannot receive the benefit.

Write down who would pay each premium and how you’ll stay informed if a policy ends or changes. Discuss whether the plan should change if one person moves out, buys the other’s share or takes over the loan. A change in the relationship or in how you share the costs is a reason to revisit the policy and title documents. The [NAIC consumer guide](https://content.naic.org/consumer/life-insurance.htm) covers the main types of individual life insurance and what to consider before choosing one.

## Talk through the house decision before you need it

Discuss whether the survivor would want to keep the home, sell it or have time to decide. Decide where each person will store the paperwork, how the other can contact the insurer and mortgage servicer, and when you’ll review the plan. If family members could have a role in settling the estate, ask a qualified attorney how to document your intentions.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can unmarried partners take out a mortgage together?**

Yes. Unmarried co-buyers can apply jointly. Before applying, discuss who will own the home, who will sign the note and how you will share the costs.

**Does a joint mortgage mean the home automatically goes to the surviving partner?**

The note identifies the borrowers. The ownership documents and applicable law determine how title transfers. Ask a local attorney to review the deed and your estate plan.

**Do both co-buyers need the same life insurance amount?**

Compare what each person could afford if they lost the other person’s contribution. The payment gap and other needs can differ, so compare coverage for each person separately.

**Can someone receive life insurance without being on the mortgage?**

The life policy’s beneficiary designation controls who receives a payable benefit, subject to the policy and applicable law. Receiving a benefit does not change who owns the home or owes the loan.
