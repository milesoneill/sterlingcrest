**Quick answer:** Term life insurance can provide money for mortgage needs if the insured person dies during the policy's coverage period and the claim meets its terms. With an individual policy, you name the beneficiary who receives that death benefit. The mortgage does not have to be paid automatically: your beneficiary can generally consider the loan alongside other household needs.

## How the policy and the mortgage fit together

A mortgage is a loan you repay over time. Term life insurance is a contract covering a person's life for a stated period. That person is called the insured. The mortgage and the life policy can support the same household goal without being the same contract. You can buy a term policy because you want your family to have money for housing if you die; that purpose does not turn it into insurance on the building or against the lender's losses if you fail to make required loan payments.

The [NAIC explains term life insurance](https://content.naic.org/article/consumer-insight-what-type-life-insurance-right-you) as coverage for a defined period that pays named beneficiaries if the insured dies during that term. A beneficiary is the person or organization named to receive the death benefit—the money payable after a covered death. The premium is what you pay for the insurance. A claim is a request for the insurer to pay after the death.

You apply for a particular policy, the insurer decides whether and on what terms to offer coverage, and the issued contract establishes when it starts. A quote is a proposed price, not proof that coverage is already active. Once the policy is in force, maintaining it requires meeting its terms, including paying required premiums.

## Your beneficiary receives money, rather than an automatic mortgage service

With ordinary individual term life insurance, you can name a family member as beneficiary. After a payable claim, that person generally decides how to use the proceeds. The insurer does not automatically manage the mortgage just because you bought coverage with the house in mind.

[Nationwide describes using term life insurance for a mortgage](https://www.nationwide.com/lc/resources/investing-and-retirement/articles/mortgage-protection). Paying off the loan is one possible use, but the family might need a different balance of debt repayment and cash on hand. A surviving partner could need time away from work, help with childcare, or money for expenses that continue even after a loan is paid off.

This differs from a lender-linked mortgage life or credit life arrangement that may direct a benefit toward the debt. If a proposal names the lender or gives the lender rights to a payment, that changes the comparison. An individual beneficiary's freedom to use money for several needs is not the same as a contract built to repay a specified loan.

## Follow one policy through the years

Suppose you have 23 years left on your mortgage and take out a 20-year term policy with a $300,000 death benefit that stays at that amount for the period. That is called a level death benefit. This is a made-up example of policy mechanics, not a suggested amount or an offer.

If you die in year eight while the policy is active and the claim meets its terms, the stated benefit is payable to the named beneficiary. Your mortgage balance at that point may be lower than it is today, but an ordinary level benefit does not shrink just because you have made loan payments. The family can consider the insurance money alongside the loan and other needs.

If you are alive when the 20-year coverage period ends, that period has done its job without a death claim. Standard term life does not usually return the premiums simply because you survived. You would also still have three scheduled mortgage years beyond the original term in this example. You need a plan for that gap, rather than assuming the policy will remain unchanged until the loan ends.

If you sell the house earlier, an individual policy may still serve other family needs. Its contract does not automatically become unnecessary just because your original housing goal changes.

## What “level” tells you—and what it leaves unanswered

A level death benefit means the insured amount stays the same during the specified period. A level premium means the required insurance payment stays the same for its guaranteed period. Those are different promises, and you should know which one a proposal is making.

The [New York Life term insurance page](https://www.newyorklife.com/products/insurance/term-life) shows that term designs can have different premium periods and renewal arrangements. For your own comparison, find both the coverage end date and the date through which the premium is guaranteed. A low initial payment does not explain what keeping the policy could cost later.

Term life is mainly death-benefit protection, rather than a cash account built up for the mortgage. Standard term does not accumulate cash value, the built-up value available in some permanent life policies, that you can borrow against. If a proposal includes a feature that returns premiums if you survive to a specified point, or another added benefit, understand it on its own terms instead of assuming that feature comes with every term policy.

The practical question is whether the amount, years, and payments work together. An attractive benefit is less useful if the payments will not fit the budget for the period you want to maintain it.

## What can happen when the term ends

Some policies allow renewal beyond an initial term, often with higher premiums. Some offer conversion, which is a contractual option to change to a permanent form of life insurance under stated rules. Permanent coverage is designed to last for life when its requirements are met. These are policy features with deadlines and limits; you should not assume every policy has them or that they remain available forever.

A plan that relies on buying entirely new insurance later is different. A future application can depend on your age, health, insurer rules, and prices at that time. Today's quote cannot establish what will be available many years from now.

Refinancing is a good reason to revisit the dates. If you replace a mortgage with a new 30-year loan, the existing life policy does not automatically restart its clock. You may have more scheduled loan years than policy years left. A lender-linked product can have different rules tied to the old loan, so that arrangement needs its own review.

Before ending existing coverage to buy something else, compare the issued contracts and effective dates. You want to understand what you would give up as well as what the new policy would add.

## Build the amount around the household, not just the original loan

The current loan balance is a useful starting point when the goal is to give your family a full-payoff option. It is not always the whole need. If the same benefit is also meant to replace income, fund childcare, or cover other debts, those jobs compete for the same dollars.

For example, a $300,000 benefit cannot be counted as both $300,000 of mortgage money and another $300,000 of income support. You would be allocating one benefit across the combined needs. Existing life insurance and savings can reduce the gap, provided they really are available for that purpose.

Also think about the financial effect of each person's death. A partner who earns less may provide childcare or manage the home, and replacing that work can add costs. A useful term plan explains whose life is insured, why that amount is needed, and how long the need may last. Those connections matter more than calling the policy mortgage protection.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does term life pay mortgage bills if I cannot work?**

Ordinary term life pays after a covered death, not simply because you lose income or become disabled. Added benefits, if included, can work differently. A rider, which is an added policy feature, that waives life insurance premiums would not mean the mortgage itself gets paid.

**Does selling my home cancel my individual term policy?**

An individual policy is generally separate from a particular home loan, but its contract controls. Selling can change your reason for having coverage without automatically ending it. Loan-linked insurance and lender assignments need separate attention.

**Can I choose someone other than the bank as beneficiary?**

With an ordinary individual policy, you can name a beneficiary under the insurer’s rules. If an assignment gives a lender rights to some of the payment, or another restriction applies, review that before assuming the entire benefit is freely payable to your family.
