**Quick answer:** A mortgage stays attached to the home when a borrower dies. A surviving co-borrower generally remains responsible for payments. If you inherited the home and weren’t on the loan, contact the mortgage servicer to find out what it needs to confirm ownership and how to keep payments current.

## First, find out whose name is on the loan

Start with the paperwork you already have. The mortgage statement tells you where to call. Your servicer is the company that manages the loan and sends the statement. It may have a different name from the lender that made the loan. The promissory note shows who agreed to repay the loan. The deed shows who owns the home. Keep those documents handy because a person can be listed on the deed without signing the note.

| Your situation | What to do first |
| --- | --- |
| You signed the mortgage loan with the person who died | Contact the servicer to confirm the next payment date, amount due and how to update account access. |
| You own or inherited an interest in the home but didn’t sign the loan | Ask what the servicer needs to recognize you as a successor in interest. That’s someone who gained an ownership interest in the home through a qualifying transfer. |
| You’re handling the estate and don’t know who owns the home yet | Locate the deed, note and most recent statement. Ask the servicer which documents it accepts from the estate representative. |

## What to ask the mortgage servicer

Call the number on a recent mortgage statement or the servicer’s verified website. Explain what happened and ask for the next steps in writing. The CFPB’s [guide for people who inherit a home](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/how-do-i-get-mortgage-information-about-a-home-i-inherited-en-217/) explains how to get account information and find out which documents you’ll need.

1. **What’s due now?** Ask for the payment amount and due date and whether the account is behind. Ask how payments can be made while ownership is being confirmed.
2. **What documents should I send?** A death certificate, an executed will or a letter from the estate’s executor may help. The right documents depend on how ownership passed and where the home is located. Ask for a secure way to submit them.
3. **What help is available if the payment is too high?** Ask the servicer about options for the specific loan. A [HUD-approved housing counselor](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/find-a-housing-counselor/) can help you prepare for that conversation. If you receive foreclosure papers, get legal help promptly.

Check where the automatic payment comes from. If it draws from an account you can’t access, arrange a payment method with the servicer and save the confirmation.

## Make a plan for the next payment

Once you know the due date, decide who will track the payment while the estate is being settled. Keep one folder for the statement, copies of what you sent and the date and name of each person you spoke with. If more than one family member is involved, agree on who will speak with the servicer so it’s clear who is following up.

If you find a life insurance policy for the borrower, the beneficiary can ask the insurer how to file a claim, what paperwork it needs, and when it expects to review the claim. Plan for the next mortgage payment using money your family can access by the due date. If that leaves a shortfall, ask the servicer about payment options while the claim is pending.

Look at other housing bills, too. If your statement shows an [escrow amount](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-an-escrow-or-impound-account-en-140/), that is money the servicer collects to pay bills such as property taxes and homeowners insurance. If those costs aren’t included in the mortgage payment, check those due dates separately. Ask the insurer how it handles the change in ownership or occupancy.

## Do you have to refinance an inherited home?

A confirmed successor can generally [keep making payments on the existing mortgage](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/data-research/research-reports/homeowners-face-problems-with-mortgage-companies-after-divorce-or-death-of-a-loved-one/) without refinancing or taking on personal responsibility for the debt. Taking over the loan in your own name is a separate step, called assumption. If you already have title, the [CFPB says](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/i-recently-inherited-a-house-the-mortgage-lender-said-its-required-to-determine-my-ability-to-repay-before-it-will-let-me-take-over-the-mortgage-loan-is-this-true-en-1939/) its ability-to-repay rules don’t require the servicer or lender to check your income before letting you take over the existing loan. Ask the servicer to explain what it needs to confirm your ownership, what assumption would involve and how to keep payments current. If personal liability or ownership is unclear, an estate attorney can help you understand the documents before you sign anything.

## How life insurance can give your family time to decide

A life insurance policy bought to help cover the mortgage can leave money for a family member to pay down the loan or cover monthly payments while deciding what to do with the home. With individual term life insurance, the insurer pays the beneficiary if the insured person dies during the covered term and the claim is payable. Check who receives the benefit, especially if the policy names the lender or has been assigned to one.

Say the home has a $220,000 loan balance and the total monthly mortgage payment is $1,850. Six months of those payments would cost $11,100. A plan to buy time could cover that amount plus other expenses the family expects. A plan to pay the loan off would start with the servicer’s payoff quote, which can differ from the balance on the statement. These hypothetical figures show why the size of the benefit depends on what you want it to do.

If you’re planning ahead, decide how long the remaining household income could cover the bills. Then check your existing policy’s amount, beneficiary and expiration date. ## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does the mortgage payment stop when the borrower dies?**

The loan generally stays in place. Call the mortgage servicer to check the due date and how to keep payments current. Ask about hardship options right away if the payment is too high.

**Can I talk to the servicer if I’m not named on the loan?**

Yes. Contact the servicer if you inherited or otherwise acquired an ownership interest in the home. Ask which documents it needs to confirm that interest and give you access to mortgage information.

**Will life insurance pay off the mortgage automatically?**

With an individual policy that pays a family member, that beneficiary typically decides how to use the proceeds. Check who receives the benefit and whether the policy has a lender assignment.

**Does the same process apply to a reverse mortgage?**

A reverse mortgage has separate repayment rules. If the home has one, tell the servicer who still lives there and ask what the loan requires after the borrower’s death.
