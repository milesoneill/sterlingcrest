**Quick answer:** Term life insurance covers a stated period, so an approaching end date is a reason to review what comes next. Some contracts offer renewal or conversion choices, with their own terms and deadlines. Check those choices alongside your current family needs and any separate plan for funeral costs before letting a deadline pass.

## Find out whether coverage is ending or a new term is being offered

At the end of a term, some policies let you renew for another period. Others end without that choice. The [New York insurance regulator's term insurance guide](https://www.dfs.ny.gov/consumers/life_insurance) explains renewal and conversion features. A renewal can come with a higher premium.

Start with the date and the choices shown in your own policy or renewal notice. Check the payment for the next period as well as any response deadline. A notice may be offering continued coverage on new payment terms, rather than simply reminding you of the old arrangement.

Some term contracts also provide a conversion option. That means changing to a form of permanent coverage under the current contract's rules. It is a different route from applying for a new policy with a separate insurer. Check for a deadline. Both renewal and conversion need to be offered in your contract before you can use them.

You do not need to make a new purchase simply because a notice arrived. You do need to know which choice the notice is describing and when you must respond.

## Revisit the reason you bought the insurance

A term policy may have been purchased while children depended on your income or while a large household debt was being paid. Years later, those needs may have changed. The amount you chose then should not become today's goal by default.

List the responsibilities that remain. For a funeral-cost discussion, include the kind of arrangements you have in mind and any money already reserved for them. Keep that purpose distinct from larger goals such as ongoing support for a spouse.

For example, paying off a mortgage can change one need without explaining who would pay a funeral bill. On the other hand, existing savings or another active policy may already address that cost. Look at both sides before assuming there is a gap.

Our [coverage-needs guide](/resources/how-much-final-expense-insurance-do-i-need) can help you organize the amount you want to discuss. It is a starting point for a conversation, not a reason to reuse an old benefit amount.

## Separate the existing policy's options from a new application

Keeping coverage through a current contract and applying for a new policy can involve different prices, dates, and steps. Put the current insurer's offer beside any new proposal so you can see what each route asks you to do.

The [Texas life insurance guide](https://www.tdi.texas.gov/pubs/consumer/cb018.html) explains term insurance and the need to compare policy terms. Your contract sets the choices for keeping coverage. A separate insurer reviews the new application you submit. Be clear about which process you are using before deciding what to end or keep.

If you apply for a new policy, give accurate details about your health and other facts as they are now. A price from when you first bought term insurance does not tell you what a new offer will cost. An old health answer should not be copied if your details have changed.

Use the actual new prices and terms. A remembered payment from years ago is not a useful basis for this choice.

## Choose an ongoing payment around your current budget

A household budget can look different near retirement or after a long period of paying for term coverage. Work with the payment you can maintain now, alongside the costs you expect to keep paying.

If an available choice to keep coverage has a different payment, connect the amount to the schedule and the period it describes. A small monthly figure, a large yearly figure, and an old payroll deduction are not directly comparable until their labels match.

You can ask about available final expense options without assuming that one product category is right for every situation. The amount, duration, terms, and payment all need to fit the need being discussed.

For example, a policy bought to cover a mortgage may be much larger than the funeral-cost gap you now face. Compare what you need and the costs of the choices you have today. A large old benefit does not mean you need to buy that amount again.

## Plan the transition with confirmed information

Start the review while there is time to read the notice and ask questions. Use the dates in your own policy and notice to plan the steps. A friend's deadline or an example in an article may differ from yours.

Before treating a new plan as complete, confirm what coverage is active and when any new coverage would start. An application or a promising quote does not, by itself, mean that an insurer has issued a policy.

Keep any separate insurance in the discussion too. Our [guide to reviewing existing life insurance](/resources/existing-life-insurance-before-burial-coverage) explains why one policy should not be evaluated in isolation from everything else you own.

The outcome may be using an option in the current contract, considering a new policy, keeping another arrangement, or gathering more facts. The immediate goal is a clear plan for the dates and expenses that matter to your family.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does a term policy always continue after its original period?**

Not always. Some contracts allow renewal for another period, and some do not. A renewal may also have a higher premium. Check the current notice and contract for the choices, payment, deadlines, and steps that apply to your policy.

**Should I buy the same coverage amount again?**

Start with your current responsibilities rather than automatically reusing a past amount. A household income need and a funeral-cost need are different goals. Include other active coverage and funds set aside when discussing a new amount.

**Can I discuss final expenses before the term policy ends?**

Yes. You can review your goals before an approaching date. Keep the current policy's details separate from any new quote, and confirm each arrangement with its insurer before making changes.
