**Quick answer:** The monthly price in a burial insurance ad is a premium for a particular example. It doesn't promise your personal rate. Check the insured person's age and other assumptions, the dollar amount of insurance, and any conditions. A proposal based on your own details is what you need to judge whether an offer fits.

## Read the sentence around the price

A large price on a small screen can become the part of an ad you remember. The words beside it may be just as important. A price might describe a stated amount of coverage, a particular example, or a starting point rather than your own cost.

Read the whole description before comparing it with another offer. Look for the payment period and the amount of insurance shown. If those details are missing from the part you saw, keep the price marked as incomplete information.

For example, a television ad may move from a small sample payment to a discussion of larger benefit amounts. That does not mean the original payment buys every amount mentioned. Link each price to the exact benefit being described.

This is a reading habit, not an assumption that every ad is misleading. Clear ads can still be misunderstood when a viewer remembers the headline but misses the explanation.

## Work out what a unit buys

If an offer describes insurance in units, find the **benefit amount for one unit**. A unit is a label in that offer, not a standard dollar amount you can carry from one insurer's ad to another.

For arithmetic only, suppose an ad defines one unit as **$1,000 of coverage**. Five units would mean $5,000 of insurance; ten would mean $10,000. These are invented benefit amounts to explain the math, not an actual policy or quoted price. The payment for one unit would not be the payment for every amount of coverage mentioned in the ad.

Write the total benefit beside the total payment and its payment period. Those three details let you compare the offer with the amount you want to explore. A payment without a coverage amount does not answer how much help your family would have.

Our [premium-versus-coverage guide](/resources/life-insurance-premium-vs-coverage-amount) explains the difference between the bill and the insurance. Once the labels are clear, request a proposal for your own details and desired amount.

## Notice who the example describes

Age, health, tobacco use, benefit amount, and policy features can affect life insurance cost. The [Texas insurance regulator's guide](https://www.tdi.texas.gov/pubs/consumer/cb018.html) explains these broad factors. A price based on someone else's details does not establish the amount you would pay.

An ad may show the assumptions in smaller print or refer you to further information. Read that context when it is available. If you cannot tell which assumptions apply, do not fill in the blanks with what you hope they mean.

For example, a family member may send you an ad because the payment looks affordable. The useful next question is whether that example matches the person who needs the coverage. Your friend's price, your parent's price, and a sample in an ad are not interchangeable.

This is also why a price you saw months ago should be checked again. Keep the company and product name with any saved image so the offer can be identified.

## Identify the business and the product

Before sharing more information or applying, know which business is collecting your request and which insurer would issue the proposed policy. Keep the company and product names with the price so you can identify the offer later.

The [North Carolina insurance regulator's shopping guide](https://www.ncdoi.gov/shopping-life-insurance) tells consumers to verify the agent and company are licensed. Use your own state insurance department for that check. Licensing is one question about the business; whether the proposed coverage fits your needs is another.

Then identify the policy type. General labels about helping with funeral costs do not give you all the terms. Read the actual proposed policy information and have any unclear point explained before choosing it.

Keep questions about price and the policy together. A clear answer should tell you what insurance is being offered, what it would cost for your details, and what steps come next.

## Use the ad to ask a precise question

Turn the part that caught your attention into one question: 'Does that payment apply to someone my age, for the amount of insurance I want?' Then identify the assumptions the ad uses and the actual proposal available to you.

If the ad gives only a payment, first find the benefit amount. If it describes units, work out the total coverage and total payment. If it gives an age or other example, compare those details with the person who would be insured. This order keeps you from judging a price before knowing what it describes.

An ad is one place to start shopping. You may compare other available options before choosing. Our [burial insurance shopping red flags](/resources/burial-insurance-shopping-red-flags) covers broader sales concerns. For the price itself, finish with the insurer and product name, your proposed benefit, the payment schedule, and the terms that apply to the offer.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does a price in an ad apply to everyone?**

Do not assume that it does. Read the description and any assumptions that accompany the example. Your own quote depends on the information used and the insurer's proposed policy.

**What if the ad gives a payment but not a benefit amount?**

You do not yet have enough information to compare it with a coverage goal. Find out how much insurance goes with the payment. A payment without its benefit amount is only part of the picture.

**Do I have to buy from the company in the ad?**

No. You can use an ad to learn about an idea and still compare other available options. Make a decision based on the proposal for you, rather than the fact that an ad first brought the subject to mind.
