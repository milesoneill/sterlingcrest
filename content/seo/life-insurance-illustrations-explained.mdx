**Quick answer:** A life insurance illustration shows how a new or existing policy could work under stated assumptions. Separate guaranteed values from values that can change, and check the payments and other details used. An illustration helps explain coverage; it is not the insurance contract or a promise that projected results will occur.

## Start with what the page is showing

A life insurance illustration can look like a long table of ages, years, payments, and values. Before focusing on the largest number, find out what each column describes. A death benefit, a cash value, and a premium are different things, even when they sit on the same row.

The [North Carolina shopping guide](https://www.ncdoi.gov/shopping-life-insurance) explains that illustrations show policy details using assumptions about future results. Those circumstances matter. The table tells you more when you know whose coverage it describes, what policy is being shown, and which payment pattern the example uses.

Not every coverage discussion needs a complex projection. If you receive an illustration, however, read it as a way to explain that proposal. A table for another amount of insurance or a different payment plan should not stand in for the option you are looking at.

## Separate a guarantee from an assumption

Guaranteed and non-guaranteed columns serve different purposes. A guaranteed value is tied to what the contract promises under its stated terms. A non-guaranteed figure uses assumptions about items that may change. A larger projected value does not carry the same meaning as a guaranteed value.

The [North Carolina Department of Insurance](https://www.ncdoi.gov/shopping-life-insurance) warns against choosing a policy solely on projected future values. It also points out that an illustration is not the policy. Read the complete proposal, including the notes on which elements are guaranteed and which are not.

For example, a projected column may show a larger value in a later year than a guaranteed column does. That gap is worth understanding. It is not extra money you can simply add to a funeral plan. First, find out what the figure represents and which assumptions would need to hold for it to occur.

## Read across a year before reading down the table

Choose one row and follow it from left to right. Find the policy year or age, the payment shown, the death benefit, and any cash value. This helps you avoid pulling one attractive figure out of a table while missing the payment that supports it.

Then move to a later row. Are payments still shown? Has the projected benefit changed? Is the value in that column guaranteed or based on assumptions? The headings and notes should answer those questions. If a heading is unclear, have it made clear before comparing it with a number in another proposal.

Cash value and death benefit deserve special care. They describe different aspects of some life policies. Don't assume both amounts would be paid together or that a displayed value is available without conditions. The actual policy determines how its values work. Our guide to [premium and coverage amounts](/resources/life-insurance-premium-vs-coverage-amount) explains the basic difference between what you pay and the insurance you buy.

## Check the payment pattern behind the example

A table is only useful if its payments match what you intend and can afford to pay. Look for when payments start, how often they are made, and how long the illustration assumes they continue. A comfortable first payment does not explain the whole pattern.

If an example shows payments stopping in a future year, do not assume that makes all later payments optional under every outcome. Find out why the table shows that pattern and whether it relies on values that can change. A planned payment shown on an illustration and a payment required by the contract are not always the same idea.

Likewise, changing a payment assumption can change what the illustration shows. When you discuss a different budget or coverage amount, use details for that revised choice. Mentally changing one number while leaving the rest of an old table untouched can create a picture that was never actually illustrated.

## Compare like assumptions before comparing results

Two illustrations can show different later values because they use different assumptions or payment patterns. The larger number alone doesn't tell you which proposal better fits your needs. First find what differs in the coverage and in the way each example was prepared.

Look at the same age or policy year. Check whether the payment schedule and insurance amount are comparable. Keep guaranteed columns separate from projected columns. Comparing a guaranteed figure in one table with a projected figure in another is a weak basis for a choice.

There may still be meaningful differences after that review. The point is to understand them rather than erase them. Our guide to [life insurance brochure questions](/resources/life-insurance-brochure-questions) covers another part of the same task: moving from a sales presentation to clear questions about the coverage itself.

## An existing policy needs current information

An illustration received when you first bought a policy describes the assumptions used then. It does not automatically describe the policy today. Actual payments and the policy's experience may have changed since that original presentation.

For coverage you already own, ask the insurer whether it can provide a current illustration and explain the figures. The [Texas life insurance guide](https://www.tdi.texas.gov/pubs/consumer/cb018.html) warns that charts can be projections rather than promises. Any updated example still needs the same careful reading of guarantees and assumptions.

Keep the full set of pages, including notes that explain the table. A single screenshot of a later value loses much of the context. The useful result is being able to explain the payment pattern, the coverage, and what could change. You don't need to predict the future to understand what the proposal is asking you to rely on.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is an illustration the same as an online quote?**

No. A quote generally gives pricing details for possible coverage. An illustration can show how payments, benefits, and values work across years under stated assumptions. Both need context, and neither replaces the policy contract.

**What if two illustrations show different payments?**

First check the payment amount, frequency, and length shown in each example. Different payment patterns can lead to different results. Have the details explained before treating a larger later value as the better offer.

**Should every final expense policy come with a large table of projected values?**

The documents used depend on the policy and how it is presented. Ask how the coverage under discussion works. If there are projections, understand their assumptions; if there are none, focus on the actual benefits, payment rules, and terms.
