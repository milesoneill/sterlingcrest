**Quick answer:** Include the amount owed on a HELOC or second mortgage if you want your family to be able to clear both home loans. If your goal is time to adjust, use the payments on both loans in your budget. Start with the HELOC balance you’ve drawn and account separately for any borrowing you actually expect.

## Put every loan secured by the home on one page

A home equity loan gives you a lump sum to repay. A home equity line of credit, or HELOC, gives you access to a credit line you can draw from within the terms of the agreement. When you already have a loan on the house, either type of borrowing can be a second mortgage. The house secures both debts, which means falling behind can put the home at risk. The [CFPB’s guide to second mortgages](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-a-second-mortgage-loan-or-junior-lien-en-105/) explains how the loans fit together.

Write down each lender or servicer, its current balance and payment, and the date the loan is scheduled to end. Then add the escrow amount if it’s part of your payment. Escrow is money collected to pay bills like property taxes and homeowners insurance. Count those costs only once if you’re totaling the two payments.

## Choose what you want the life insurance to do

Mortgage protection life insurance is life insurance bought with the household’s home debt in mind. With an individual policy that pays a family member, the proceeds can give that person room to decide what to do with the loans. Review the policy if it names the lender or if part of the benefit has been assigned to a lender.

| Your goal | What to put on the worksheet |
| --- | --- |
| Pay off both loans | Get a payoff quote from each servicer. These quotes show what it costs to clear each loan by a given date. |
| Keep the payments manageable for a while | Add both payments and the home costs you pay outside escrow. Compare that total with what the household could pay from its remaining income. |
| Cover a renovation you’re already committed to | Separate the balance you’ve already borrowed from the additional amount you reasonably expect to draw. Revisit the plan once the project is complete. |

A payoff quote can include interest through the payoff date and any applicable charges. The balance on a statement is a useful planning number. Use the quotes when someone is ready to pay off the debt.

## Use the HELOC balance you’ve drawn as a starting point

Here’s a hypothetical example. Your first mortgage balance is $245,000. Your HELOC has an $80,000 limit, but you’ve drawn only $22,000. The balances currently owed add up to $267,000. If a signed renovation contract means you expect to draw $15,000 more, you could also model $282,000 as a later scenario. The two numbers serve different purposes.

If your goal is several months of payments, check how each loan bills you. Suppose the total first-mortgage payment is $1,820 and the HELOC bill is $180. Together they come to $2,000 per month, or $12,000 for six months. The amounts are hypothetical and leave out any housing costs billed separately. If the HELOC payment changes, rerun the example with the new bill.

## Check when the HELOC moves into repayment

A HELOC’s draw period is the time when you can borrow against the available line. In the repayment period, you typically pay down what you’ve borrowed under the loan terms. The [CFPB’s HELOC guide](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-a-home-equity-line-of-credit-heloc-en-107/) explains why a bill can increase when repayment begins. Ask your lender when that change is expected and what it means for your loan.

Keep the two dates visible if the first mortgage and HELOC end years apart. A policy timed to the main mortgage could expire while the other loan still has a balance. That matters if your family needs coverage for the second loan through its final payment. Our [mortgage protection coverage guide](/resources/how-much-mortgage-protection-insurance-do-i-need) walks through choosing a payoff or payment goal.

## Review what you have before applying

Compare the combined debt with any life insurance you already own. Check the death benefit, the expiration date and who receives it. If the benefit was originally meant to support children or replace income, decide how much of it you’re also counting toward the loans. Counting the same dollars for both goals can hide a shortfall.

The next time you draw a substantial amount, switch repayment periods or pay off either loan, update the worksheet. If the new numbers leave a gap, [book a brief phone consultation](/book) and bring both statements so you can compare coverage for the debt you expect to carry.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Should I count the full HELOC credit limit as debt?**

Start with the amount currently drawn. Add any borrowing you actually expect as a separate scenario. The available limit tells you how much you may be able to borrow under the agreement.

**Does one life insurance policy have to be assigned to each lender?**

An individual policy can name a family member as beneficiary, who can then decide how to use a payable benefit. Review any lender assignment already on a policy before relying on it for both debts.

**Do I have to buy more life insurance if I take out a HELOC?**

Check your current coverage and your goal first. A higher home balance can leave a gap, but the amount depends on the policy you have and how much of the debt you want it to help with.
