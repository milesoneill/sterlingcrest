**Quick answer:** Start with what you want the money to accomplish: pay off the mortgage, help with payments for a period, or support a wider household plan. Add the costs for that goal, then subtract funds truly set aside for it. Your loan balance is one input. Existing life insurance, other family needs, and the years of support matter too.

## Choose a housing goal before choosing a dollar amount

A full-payoff goal starts with the amount needed to settle the loan, using a current figure when you reach a real policy choice. The balance on an old statement is useful for an early estimate, but it is not a current payoff quote.

A transition goal is different. The household might want enough money to keep making payments while deciding whether to stay, move, or sell. That is a support fund for a set period. It does not mean you need to insure the entire loan.

[New York Life explains using life insurance for mortgage needs](https://www.newyorklife.com/articles/utilize-life-insurance), such as the idea of linking coverage to an unpaid mortgage. Your own goal still needs to be explicit.

Write the goal in one sentence: “We want the surviving household to have the option to pay off this loan,” or “We want two years of housing costs while choices are made.” These are example planning goals, not recommendations. Two households with identical balances can choose different goals because their income, savings, and plans differ.

## An example full-payoff calculation

Here is an example with made-up numbers to show the math. It is not a premium quote or a suggested policy amount:

| Planning item | Example amount |
| --- | ---: |
| Mortgage amount included in the goal | $240,000 |
| Other household support included in this plan | $36,000 |
| Total listed need | $276,000 |
| Savings set aside for these needs | −$20,000 |
| Existing life insurance set aside for these needs | −$100,000 |
| Example remaining gap | $156,000 |

The calculation is $240,000 + $36,000 − $20,000 − $100,000 = $156,000. It is a starting estimate, not an application amount. It leaves out items the household has not chosen to include.

The key step is to decide how you would use each source of funds. The $100,000 existing policy cannot also be counted in full against a separate income-replacement plan. Likewise, savings reserved for an emergency fund cannot be used twice. Write down what each resource is supposed to cover.

The [NYLAARP guide to working out life insurance needs](https://www.nylaarp.newyorklife.com/Learning-Center/Exploring-coverage/Understanding-term-coverage-and-your-options) explains how to weigh needs against funds you can use. The figures above are original examples of that planning method.

## For payment support, use the real housing budget

Suppose a different example household wants 24 months of housing support. If the chosen housing budget is $2,000 per month, the starting calculation is $2,000 × 24 = $48,000. That is an illustration of a support fund, not a claim that $48,000 will protect every home or replace the whole mortgage.

Decide what the $2,000 includes. Principal and interest alone may leave out property taxes, homeowners insurance, association dues, utilities, and maintenance. If a mortgage payment includes an escrow amount, do not add the same taxes and insurance again.

Also test the budget after one household member's death. Some expenses may fall, while paid childcare, transportation, or help around the house may rise. The missing help can include unpaid work as well as wages. A spreadsheet copied from today's spending will not necessarily show tomorrow's gap.

A payment-support plan may leave the loan balance largely unpaid at the end of the chosen period. That is not an arithmetic mistake; it is a feature of the goal. Name the plan for after that period: continued earnings, a planned sale, other savings, or a later choice still to be made.

## Count existing funds carefully and revisit the time frame

For each life policy you already have, think about the insured person, beneficiary, amount, and end date. A policy on someone else's life does not address the same death scenario. A benefit paid to a different person may not be on hand for the housing goal you are working out.

Workplace coverage also deserves a separate note. Check the plan rather than expecting the amount will remain in force through every job change or until the mortgage ends. [Guardian's life insurance overview](https://www.guardianlife.com/life-insurance) discusses life insurance needs and the limits of relying solely on employer coverage.

Do not treat the home's full equity as ready cash without thinking it through. Accessing that value may require a sale or borrowing, which can conflict with a goal of staying in the house. The same practical question applies to any asset: would the household actually use it for this goal at that time?

Finally, put a date beside the estimate. Review after refinancing, a major principal payment, a change in family income, or a new dependent. A reasonable amount today can become mismatched later. The calculation should help you explain the need, then review any policy choice and its cost with an insurance professional.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Should I just insure the entire original loan?**

Use the current goal rather than simply copying the original loan amount. The unpaid debt may have changed, and the household may need either a payoff option or a more limited period of support. Existing funds also affect the gap.

**Should both borrowers have the same amount?**

Not always. Work out what each person adds and how their death would affect the surviving household. Income and unpaid work can create different needs. A shared mortgage balance alone does not show that impact.

**Does this estimate tell me the monthly premium?**

No. A benefit calculation is not a price calculation. Premiums depend on the policy and underwriting factors. The example does not show eligibility, affordability, or the amount an insurer would approve.
