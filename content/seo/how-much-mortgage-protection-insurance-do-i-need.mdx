**Quick answer:** The amount depends on what you want a death benefit to do for your family. Start with a mortgage payoff goal or a period of payment support, add other needs the same money must cover, and subtract resources genuinely set aside for those needs. Your loan balance matters, but it does not show the whole household gap by itself.

## First decide what you mean by protecting the mortgage

Mortgage protection through life insurance means arranging money that can help with the home loan after an insured person dies. A death benefit is the amount the insurer pays after a covered death. With an individual life policy, the beneficiary—the person you name to receive that benefit—can generally consider the mortgage alongside other expenses.

That creates more than one sensible planning question. Do you want your family to have the option to pay off the loan? Would you want enough money to keep making payments for a while? Or is the mortgage one part of a broader plan to replace your income and unpaid work?

[New York Life discusses using life insurance for a mortgage](https://www.newyorklife.com/articles/utilize-life-insurance). The relevant amount starts with your intended result, rather than a universal multiple of income or a number printed on the original loan papers. Two families can owe the same amount and need different support because their income, savings, dependents, and plans differ.

## For a payoff goal, start with the debt you would want cleared

If you want the family to be able to pay off the mortgage, use the remaining loan amount as the starting figure. An old statement or the original borrowing amount may no longer reflect what you owe. As you approach a real decision, the servicer—the company managing your loan account—can provide a [current payoff figure](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-a-payoff-amount-and-is-it-the-same-as-my-current-balance-en-205/). It can include interest—the cost of borrowing money—through the payoff date. That can make it different from the unpaid borrowed amount, or principal balance, shown on a statement.

Paying off the mortgage would not make the house free to keep. Property taxes, homeowners insurance, maintenance, utilities, and any association dues can continue. Your family may also need money to manage the household while adjusting to the loss of your income or work.

You do not have to add every possible future expense to a mortgage-only plan. You do need to be clear about what you have included. If you are choosing a debt payoff amount while assuming another resource will cover living costs, name that resource and make sure it has not already been allocated somewhere else.

## A worked example that keeps the money from being counted twice

Here is a made-up example of a household choosing a full-payoff option. The numbers show the method, not an amount you should buy or an insurance price quote.

The family wants the option to clear the mortgage and keep some money available while the surviving partner adjusts. They count only savings and existing life insurance deliberately allocated to those same needs:

| Part of this hypothetical plan | Amount |
| --- | ---: |
| Mortgage included in the payoff goal | $240,000 |
| Other household support included | +$36,000 |
| Total identified need | $276,000 |
| Savings reserved for these needs | −$20,000 |
| Existing life benefit on the person being considered, payable to the intended family member | −$100,000 |
| Starting coverage gap | $156,000 |

The calculation is **$240,000 + $36,000 − $20,000 − $100,000 = $156,000**. The table keeps the needs and the resources separate so each dollar has one job.

That figure is an estimate to discuss, not a claim that an insurer will issue that exact amount. It also depends on the assumptions being true. If the existing $100,000 policy is already meant to cover another need, it cannot be subtracted here in full as well. If the savings are an emergency reserve the family wants to preserve, they should not be treated as freely available mortgage money.

The [NYLAARP guide to estimating life insurance needs](https://www.nylaarp.newyorklife.com/Learning-Center/Exploring-coverage/Understanding-term-coverage-and-your-options) describes this broad needs-minus-resources approach. The usefulness comes from making the assumptions visible.

## A payment-support goal uses a different calculation

You might prefer to plan for a period of housing support while your family decides whether to stay, sell, or change its spending. That does not necessarily require enough insurance to clear the full loan, but it leaves a different responsibility at the end of the support period.

For a simple example, suppose you choose two years of housing support and work out a $2,000 monthly housing budget. The starting calculation is $2,000 × 24 months, or $48,000. Those are made-up figures, not a claim that two years or $48,000 is enough for your household.

The important work is defining the monthly budget. Principal and interest are the loan payment components that repay what you borrowed and pay the borrowing cost. Your total payment may also include money collected for taxes and insurance through an escrow account, a separate account used to pay those bills. If you start with that total, avoid adding those same taxes and insurance again. Then consider costs outside the mortgage payment, such as utilities or maintenance.

At the end of the two years, the mortgage may still be largely outstanding. The plan needs an answer for what happens next: continued income, other funds, a sale, or another deliberate choice. A support fund buys time; it does not guarantee a permanent housing solution.

## Check how available your existing resources really are

Existing life insurance can reduce a new coverage need, but first confirm whose life it covers, who receives it, how much it pays, and when it ends. Insurance on your partner's life does not pay because you die. A benefit payable to someone outside the plan may not be usable for the housing goal you have in mind.

Workplace coverage needs particular care. Its amount and continuation can depend on the employment plan, so do not assume it will remain unchanged through a job move or all the years left on the mortgage. [Guardian discusses life insurance needs and employer coverage](https://www.guardianlife.com/life-insurance). Check the actual plan before counting on it for a long-term goal.

Home equity is the home's value minus the debts secured against it. It is different from cash in the bank. Accessing the value tied up in your house can require a sale or borrowing, which may conflict with the goal of helping your family stay there. The planning question is not merely whether an asset exists, but whether your family would realistically use it for this purpose at the time needed.

## Test the amount against the household after a death

Today's budget assumes everyone is still there. After a death, some costs may fall, while others may rise. A surviving partner could need paid childcare, transportation help, or fewer work hours. The financial contribution lost is not always just a salary.

Work through each adult's situation rather than copying the same number onto both people. A lower-earning partner may provide work the household would have to replace. One person may already have individual coverage that lasts long enough, while the other's insurance ends sooner.

Finally, put the estimate beside the years you want protection and the premium—the insurance payment—you could maintain. A benefit calculation tells you about a need; it does not tell you what a policy will cost or whether you qualify. Revisit the estimate after refinancing, a major debt payment, a new dependent, or a significant income change so the numbers continue to describe your household.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Should I insure the original loan amount or the current balance?**

For a full-payoff goal, the current amount needed to settle the debt is more useful than automatically copying the original loan. If the policy also needs to support living costs, those needs should be included separately rather than hidden inside the mortgage figure.

**Does PMI reduce the life insurance amount I need?**

Private mortgage insurance, or PMI, protects the lender against certain losses if the borrower fails to make required loan payments; it is not a death benefit for your family. Do not subtract it as though it were money available after your death.

**Does the calculation tell me what my premium will be?**

No. The coverage amount is only one pricing factor. The policy and coverage period matter, as does underwriting: the insurer's review of your application and relevant health and other facts. These can affect what is offered and what it costs. The worked examples are planning arithmetic, not insurance quotes or promises of approval.
