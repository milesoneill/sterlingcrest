**Quick answer:** Divorce is a reason to review your life insurance, especially the owner, named beneficiaries, and payment source. It doesn't tell you by itself what changes are permitted or required. Start with the policy records and any legal obligations, then decide whether the coverage still fits your family and funeral-cost goals.

## Find out what you own before changing anything

A shared household may have had several policies, more than one payer, or a folder of old statements. First, find the current insurer and the person insured under each policy. Then check who owns it and who is named to receive a payable death benefit.

Those are separate roles. Being the person insured does not answer every ownership question, and paying a bill does not automatically give someone all the rights in a contract. Keep the company's record as your starting point.

The [California life insurance guide](https://www.insurance.ca.gov/01-consumers/105-type/95-guides/07-life/life-ins-guide.cfm) recommends reviewing policies as needs and finances change. That is useful after a divorce too. The goal is a clear picture of your current coverage. Read the contract before making changes to it.

A useful note for each policy includes the company, policy number, owner, insured person, named beneficiaries, and the account used for payments. Mark anything you have not confirmed. Our guide to [organizing final expense insurance records](/resources/organize-final-expense-insurance-records) offers a place to start if the paperwork is spread across several folders.

## Treat legal obligations and insurer records as separate checks

A divorce agreement, a court order, and an insurance form can each matter, but they do different jobs. Do not assume that reading one of them explains the full effect of the others.

If an agreement refers to life insurance, have that part explained by the lawyer helping with the divorce. The question may concern who must maintain coverage, who may own it, or who must be named. A general insurance article cannot determine those rights for your family.

At the same time, find out what the insurer's record shows now and how it handles a requested change. The [Texas life insurance guide](https://www.tdi.texas.gov/pubs/consumer/cb018.html) explains the beneficiary's role in receiving a death benefit. Check the names and details in your own record.

Avoid acting on a blanket claim that divorce always removes an ex-spouse, or that an old name can never be affected by divorce. The legal result can depend on the policy and the law that applies. Work through the actual documents before signing a change request. That keeps a routine record update from being mistaken for legal advice.

## Revisit the job you want the coverage to do

After a household separates, the purpose of an existing policy may need a closer look. Some coverage may relate to ongoing family support. Other coverage may be intended for funeral and end-of-life expenses. Keep those goals distinct so one small policy is not expected to do every job.

For the final expense part, think about who would arrange your funeral and which costs you want funded. There may be different people involved now than when you first bought the policy. You may also have changed your own wishes about burial, cremation, or a service.

For example, someone may have assumed a spouse would handle arrangements and know where every record was kept. After divorce, a sibling or adult child may be the person they would contact. The task is to make the current plan clear, rather than leave that person to work out how an old plan was meant to work.

You can [estimate a final expense coverage goal](/resources/how-much-final-expense-insurance-do-i-need) using the costs and funds that actually apply now. An existing policy may still have a useful role. A review does not automatically mean replacing it or buying more.

## Make sure the payment plan still works

A policy can be easy to overlook when it was paid from a joint account. Check the payment source directly, including the next due date and who receives notices. An agreement about who will pay is one thing. Proof that the insurer has received the premium is another.

If an account will close, arrange a payment update the policy allows through the insurer's own process. Keep a record of the request and check how future payments will be collected. Don't assume that changing a bank account elsewhere updates the policy too.

The [Texas life insurance guide](https://www.tdi.texas.gov/pubs/consumer/cb018.html) explains why maintaining payments matters. Its state rules apply in Texas. Check your own policy and state rules for the terms that apply to you.

Any new payment should fit your own ongoing budget. Housing and everyday expenses may look quite different after divorce. Start with a payment you can afford now. An old idea of what insurance should cost may no longer fit the bills you need to pay.

## Finish the review with clear next steps

Finish with three decisions rather than a pile of unanswered questions:

| What you found | Next action |
| --- | --- |
| An order or agreement refers to life insurance | Have the legal obligation explained before changing ownership or beneficiaries |
| The insurer's record or payment account is out of date | Use the insurer's change process and keep its reply |
| Your own funeral-cost goal is no longer funded as intended | Review existing coverage and any remaining need within your current budget |

For example, a policy may still have the right coverage amount while its payment comes from an account you plan to close. That is a payment task. It is not, by itself, a reason to buy a new policy. A different situation is a policy intended for ongoing support that leaves your own funeral costs unplanned. That calls for a separate discussion of the goal.

Keep the company's reply when an allowed change is done. Tell a trusted person where the current policy information is stored; private divorce papers do not need to become part of a shared funeral-planning folder.

You do not need every issue settled before a first coverage call. Explain which legal questions remain open so they are not treated as resolved. The useful result is a clear next action, whether that is an insurer update, a legal question, or comparing available final expense options.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Should I cancel a policy that names my former spouse?**

Do not cancel it based only on the name you see. First check the owner's rights, the policy terms, and any legal obligations. A permitted beneficiary change and cancellation are different actions with different consequences.

**Does changing my will update my life insurance?**

Use the insurer's record and change process for the policy. Do not treat a change to another estate document as confirmation that an insurance designation has been updated. Legal guidance can help explain how the documents work together.

**Can a consultation help if I am unsure whether I need new coverage?**

Yes. You can discuss what you want funeral-cost coverage to do and what you already have. Questions about a court order or legal rights still need the appropriate professional; you do not need to arrive with a decision to replace a policy.
