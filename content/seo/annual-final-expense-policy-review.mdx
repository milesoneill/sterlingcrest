**Quick answer:** A yearly final expense review can begin with your current documents, contact details, and a note about any family goals that have changed. Ask the insurer to confirm unclear records or terms. Keep a dated list of answers and open questions. The review itself does not require a purchase or automatically change your policy.

## Choose a time you can keep

A yearly reminder is a personal planning habit, not a rule set by this checklist. Pick a date that works for you and gather the documents you already have. Start with a small task, such as checking where the current copy is kept. Avoid turning a review into an automatic decision that you need to buy something new.

## Check the records you rely on

Read the names and contact details on the material in your folder. Ask the insurer how to confirm anything that is unclear or no longer matches your own notes. The Texas regulator’s [life insurance guide](https://www.tdi.texas.gov/pubs/consumer/cb018.html) provides general background. Your actual record still needs to be checked with the company involved rather than inferred from a guide.

## Ask whether your planning questions have changed

Write down any new concern or family goal you want to discuss. Keep a preference separate from a change to an insurance document. The New York regulator’s [consumer guide](https://www.dfs.ny.gov/consumers/life_insurance) can help with basic questions. It does not decide whether a change is suitable for your own policy or show that a personal note changed its terms.

## Keep a dated list of follow-ups

Use three headings: checked, question sent, and answer still needed. Record the document or person behind each response. If you receive a new page, label it without losing track of the earlier copy. A review can be useful even when its result is a short list of facts to confirm rather than a new financial decision.

## Separate a record update from a purchase decision

Suppose you have moved since you last looked at your insurance folder. A useful first step is to ask the insurer how to check the contact details it has on file. That question concerns an existing record. It does not, by itself, show that you need a different policy or more coverage.

You might also find that a family goal has changed. Write down what is different and the question it raises. For example, you may want a clearer picture of how your current planning relates to the costs you hope to address. Keep that discussion separate from any decision to stop or replace coverage. The actual policy details and your circumstances matter.

If someone mentions a new offer, avoid treating the word new as a reason to act. The Texas regulator's guide urges care about replacing life insurance. This checklist does not compare products or recommend a replacement. Use it to identify the facts you need to understand, and seek the right explanation before making any change.

## End the review with a small action list

A review is easier to finish when each open point has a clear next step. You might use the following prompts and leave out anything that does not apply.

- [ ] **Records.** I know where the current policy material is kept.
- [ ] **Contact details.** I have identified any information to confirm with the insurer.
- [ ] **Family goals.** I can describe a question or concern that has changed.
- [ ] **Follow-up.** I know which company or person can answer the outstanding point.
- [ ] **Date.** I can tell what I checked and what remains unanswered.
- [ ] **Next review.** I have chosen a reminder if that would help me stay organized.

A short result is fine. You may find that nothing needs attention beyond confirming one detail. ## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is a yearly review mandatory?**

This page suggests a personal habit, not a requirement. Choose a way to keep track of questions that works for you.

**Does a review mean I should replace my policy?**

No. Understanding your records and goals is separate from deciding whether any change is suitable.

**Can I update an insurer’s record by changing my own worksheet?**

Do not assume that you can. Ask the company about its own process and how to confirm the record.

**What if nothing important has changed?**

You can still note the date and any details you checked. There is no need to invent a reason for a new purchase.

**What if I find an old policy but do not know its current status?**

Use the company information on the document as a starting point for asking how to confirm the record. Do not treat an old page alone as proof of current coverage or assume that it has ended. Keep the status marked as unconfirmed until the appropriate company has explained it. A general article cannot verify an individual policy.
