**Quick answer:** The owner controls a life insurance policy, while the insured is the person whose life it covers. The beneficiary is named to receive the death benefit. One person can fill more than one role. For final expense planning, list who fills each role so your family knows who can request changes and where a claim begins.

## Start with the three names on the policy

A life insurance policy can involve several people even when it covers only one life. The owner holds the contract, and the insured is the person whose death can give rise to a death claim. The beneficiary is named to receive the money due under the contract. This may be a person or an organization.

The [NAIC's consumer overview](https://content.naic.org/consumer/life-insurance.htm) explains that life insurance is designed to pay named beneficiaries when the insured dies. The buyer is often the insured. But those roles can differ. Your family might call a plan “Mom's insurance,” but that phrase tells you little about who owns it. The names on the contract are what you need.

## The owner handles changes while the insured is alive

The owner can generally manage the contract, subject to its terms and any limits. That may include a request to change who will receive the money. The owner may also ask about values or submit a request to end the contract. Some changes may not be allowed, so a request is not proof that a change took effect.

[MetLife's explanation of a policyholder](https://www.metlife.com/stories/benefits/what-is-a-policyholder/) separates the owner’s role from the insured person’s role. On an individual life contract, find the owner field rather than assuming the person who gets the mail owns it. An address or access to a folder can help you find records, but they do not replace the insurer's owner record.

## Paying the premium does not explain every role

One person may pay the bill while someone else owns the policy. When you look at a bank statement, it shows where a payment came from. It does not show who can make changes or who gets the death benefit.

This can prevent a common mix-up. A son who helps pay for a parent’s policy may expect the insurer to discuss every detail with him. The company may still need the owner’s consent. When you call, explain which role you think you hold and ask what proof the company needs. Use a verified company contact before you send private records. Do not send them to a stranger to prove you paid a bill.

## Consent and control are separate questions

If you want a policy on someone else, that person needs to be involved. Do not use this as a way to buy coverage in secret or answer another adult's health questions for them. The insurer must address consent and insurable interest. Insurable interest is the required stake in the person’s life. The company also has rules for applying.

Once a policy is in place, the insured and owner may have different tasks. The insured may give health facts when applying, while the owner may handle later requests to change the contract. A parent and adult child can start with three questions. Who will own the policy? Who will answer the health questions? Who will pay the bills? Agree on the roles before assuming them.

## Being named for the money does not make you the owner

Being named as a beneficiary does not, by itself, give someone control while the insured is alive. The two fields serve different purposes.

The name of the person set to receive the money also needs care. [MetLife describes revocable and irrevocable beneficiaries](https://www.metlife.com/stories/benefits/beneficiary/), with different rules for changes. Do not assume you can change any name with a quick phone call. If a limit applies, the insurer can explain what it needs. Some plans involve trusts or divorce orders; others involve a change of owner or a person who cannot act alone. These need advice based on the facts. A general article cannot settle them.

## An example: three roles in one family

Here is a made-up example of a family planning for a mother’s final expenses. The mother owns the policy and is the insured. She names her adult daughter as beneficiary. Two people fill three roles, with the mother managing the contract. The daughter is named for a future death benefit.

Now change one fact. Suppose the daughter is both owner and beneficiary. The mother knows about this, and the insurer has accepted it. The mother is still the insured, but the daughter now holds the owner’s role. That is a different arrangement, even if both families call it “Mom's burial insurance.” The example explains the roles. The right setup depends on the people and the facts.

## Write down the roles before a call

Write a line for each role. List the owner, the insured, and each person named for the death benefit. Add the company and policy number to private records. Note when you last checked the details. Use the insurer's current records to resolve anything unclear.

For a new final expense discussion, start with who needs the coverage and what you hope it will help your family pay for. You do not need to settle each owner detail in advance. Sterling Crest can explain the questions involved in comparing available coverage. An insurer must handle changes to its own records, while legal or tax effects need advice from a qualified professional.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can the owner and insured be the same person?**

Yes. A person often owns a policy covering their own life and names someone else as beneficiary. A separate owner is possible if accepted. Check the fields on the contract.

**Does being the beneficiary let me cancel the policy?**

Being named for the money alone does not give you the right to cancel. The insurer must confirm who holds the owner’s rights and whether any restrictions apply.

**Can one person own a policy and also be its beneficiary?**

That can occur when the policy covers another person in a valid arrangement. Before applying, make sure consent, insurable interest, and the roles are clear. The insurer must accept the setup.
