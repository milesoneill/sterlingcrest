**Quick answer:** An automatic premium loan is a policy provision that can borrow against available policy value to pay an overdue premium when its conditions are met. It can help prevent a lapse, but it creates a loan with interest. It is not a free premium or a withdrawal from your bank account.

## The feature uses policy value, not bank autopay

For final-expense planning, the important question is how much benefit remains available after any policy debt. Review your existing coverage and savings before assuming the face amount on an old schedule is the amount your family would receive. A policy kept active through borrowing can still need attention even when no premium seems to leave your checking account.

Bank autopay sends money from an authorized account to the insurer. An automatic premium loan instead uses a borrowing provision within an eligible cash-value policy. The premium may be paid through a loan even though your bank statement shows no payment.

The [New York Department of Financial Services life insurance guide](https://www.dfs.ny.gov/consumers/life_insurance) describes the provision and its election by the policy owner. Its state-specific requirements are not a substitute for the terms and law applying to your own contract. Check whether the feature is offered, whether it is elected, and what triggers it.

Not every life policy has cash value or an automatic premium loan provision. Do not assume the feature exists on a term policy, a new policy with little value, or an older contract whose available value has already been used.

## Find out when borrowing begins

Read the provision's trigger alongside the premium due date and grace-period terms. The feature should not be treated as a reason to ignore a late-payment notice. Confirm whether the company has already created a loan or is only warning that it may do so.

Ask for the date, amount, and premium covered by any automatic borrowing. If you expected a bank debit, separately investigate why it did not happen. An automatic premium loan might address the missed premium without correcting a canceled debit authorization or an outdated bank account.

This distinction can explain a confusing statement: the policy shows a premium paid, yet the bank shows no withdrawal and the loan balance has grown. Those records may describe the same transaction rather than an extra payment you made.

## Interest changes the longer-term picture

A premium loan is still a loan. Review the contract's interest terms, how interest is billed or added to the balance, and how outstanding debt affects available values. Several small premium loans can create a larger obligation over time.

[MetLife's whole life servicing FAQs](https://www.metlife.com/support-and-manage/current-customers/individual-life-insurance/products/wl-faqs/) provide an example of an insurer explaining automatic premium loans, interest, and reductions to cash surrender value and the death benefit. Those FAQs concern its policies; your insurer's provision may differ. They illustrate why the benefit cannot be understood by looking only at the original face amount.

Suppose, hypothetically, an insurer reports a $12,000 face amount and $700 of policy debt, including interest. If that debt is deducted from the benefit under the contract, it reduces the amount available for the family's goal. Obtain the actual payable-benefit figure rather than treating this example as your calculation.

## Available value is finite

The insurer can use only the value available under the loan provision. If the amount is insufficient, another premium may not be funded in the same way. Existing loans and interest can affect what remains available.

That is why an automatic premium loan should not be described as a permanent solution to an unaffordable payment. Ask what happens if borrowing continues: whether a later premium could go unpaid, how notices would be sent, and what choices are still available. A current statement is more useful than an assumption that past borrowing can continue indefinitely.

Our guide to [life insurance policy loans](/resources/final-expense-life-insurance-policy-loans) covers borrowing more broadly. Automatic premium loans deserve their own review because they can be triggered by a missed payment rather than a fresh request for spending money.

## Ask how payments will be applied

If you want to stop further borrowing, first establish how to resume premiums and how to handle the existing loan. Sending the usual premium amount does not necessarily repay accumulated debt as well. Ask how a payment should be labeled and confirm its application afterward.

Likewise, ending an automatic premium loan election does not by itself erase an existing loan or guarantee future coverage. Understand the next premium obligation before changing the setting. Keep a copy of the requested change and the insurer's confirmation.

If the debt or continuing premium raises a question about whether the policy still meets your funeral-cost goal, a [phone consultation about your existing coverage](/book) can focus on that gap. Review the existing contract before considering a replacement; a new application is a separate decision with its own conditions.

## Keep notices connected to your coverage plan

Store premium notices, loan statements, and the current benefit information together. If a family member helps manage paperwork, use the insurer's authorization process so that person receives only the access or notices actually permitted.

The [NAIC buyer's guide](https://content.naic.org/sites/default/files/publication-lig-lp-consumer-life.pdf) recommends reviewing life insurance as needs and policy values change. For this feature, a review should answer three practical questions: is the policy active, what amount is owed, and what payment is required next? Add the net benefit to your final-expense planning records after the insurer confirms it.

Keep [missed-payment questions](/resources/missed-life-insurance-premium-payment) separate from assumptions about borrowing. A notice may involve a bank problem, a premium loan, or a lapse; those are different situations with different next steps.

## Do not confuse a temporary safeguard with paid-up coverage

A policy supported by premium loans is not automatically paid up. Future premiums may still be required, and the loan continues under the contract until handled through its permitted options. The absence of a bank debit proves neither that the policy needs no attention nor that it has ended.

Ask the insurer to explain any tax consequences before surrendering or allowing a policy with debt to terminate. Individual tax treatment requires its own review; a loan statement is not a tax calculation.
