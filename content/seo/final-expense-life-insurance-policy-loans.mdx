**Quick answer:** A final expense life policy may let you borrow against available cash value if its terms allow it. A policy loan creates debt and interest—the charge for borrowing. Unpaid debt can reduce the death benefit, meaning the money payable after a covered death, or put coverage at risk if it grows too large. Understand those effects before borrowing from your family's final expense plan.

## What you are borrowing against

If your final expense policy has cash value, you may have seen a loan amount on an annual statement. Cash value is value built up within certain life insurance contracts while you're alive. A policy loan uses available value as security for borrowing. It isn't simply an early payment of the death benefit, and the name final expense insurance doesn't guarantee that a loan is available.

[Guardian's guide to life insurance loans](https://www.guardianlife.com/life-insurance/loans) explains the connection between the contract, available value, and borrowing. The stated amount of life insurance, the cash value, and the available loan amount can all differ. A policy with a substantial death benefit may have a much smaller amount available to borrow.

Timing matters too. Paying premiums doesn't mean each payment becomes an equal amount of loan value. A new policy may need time to build enough value, and its terms determine what you can request. [Guardian's discussion of when borrowing becomes available](https://www.guardianlife.com/life-insurance/withdraw/how-soon) explains that cash value plays a central role. If you're thinking about a loan for a bill due now, use the insurer's current available amount. A future value shown in an illustration isn't money you can necessarily borrow today.

## How interest changes what remains for your family

Interest is the charge for using the loan. The contract sets how it applies, and unpaid interest may be added to the debt. That means the balance can grow even if you never borrow another dollar. [Protective's policy loan explanation](https://www.protective.com/resources-for-you/learn-by-topic/life-insurance/basics/borrowing-money-from-life-insurance) describes how unpaid principal—the amount borrowed—and interest can affect the eventual death benefit.

Here's a hypothetical arithmetic example, not a quote or a prediction about a policy. Suppose a policy would otherwise pay $15,000 after a covered death. At that time, it has a $1,000 loan balance and $50 of unpaid interest. If those are the only deductions, the net benefit would be $13,950: $15,000 minus $1,000 minus $50.

The difference matters if your family is expecting the full face amount, meaning the stated amount of insurance. A folder showing $15,000 may no longer describe what would be payable after debt is deducted. The actual amount depends on the contract, current balance, interest, and claim circumstances. Repayments can change the balance too, so the important figure is what's outstanding at the relevant time, not just the amount you originally received.

## Why keeping premiums current may not be enough

Premiums and loan payments do different jobs. The premium maintains insurance under its terms; a loan payment addresses debt and interest. If you continue the same automatic bank draft after borrowing, don't assume that the draft now takes care of both. You need to understand how the insurer applies each payment.

A loan can affect more than the amount paid after death. If debt and interest grow too large in relation to the policy value, the contract can be at risk of lapsing, which means ending. The calculation and any notice requirements depend on the contract and applicable rules. A loan without a conventional monthly repayment schedule still needs attention.

That is why a loan notice deserves a look even when your premium bill seems unchanged. The insurer may be describing a separate issue caused by accumulated debt. If it gives an amount needed to keep the policy in force, the relevant questions are what that amount addresses and what deadline applies. Paying a regular premium may not fix the debt problem.

You don't need to become an insurance accountant to follow this. Keeping the current loan balance, interest information, and policy-status notices together gives you a more accurate picture than looking only at whether the last premium cleared your bank.

## A loan, a withdrawal, and surrender are different choices

People sometimes say they want to “take money out” of a life policy. That phrase can mean several actions with different results. A loan creates debt connected to the contract. A withdrawal removes value under a provision that permits it. Surrender voluntarily ends the policy in exchange for any net surrender value payable. The insurer needs to know which action you're requesting, and you need to know what would happen afterward.

Some contracts may also offer an [automatic premium loan provision](https://www.metlife.com/support-and-manage/current-customers/individual-life-insurance/products/wl-faqs/). When that provision is in effect and there's enough available value, it can use borrowing to pay a missed premium under its terms. The premium is being funded with debt rather than becoming free, so the resulting loan and interest still matter to the policy.

When comparing actions, focus on the effect on available value, future premiums, and the benefit you want your family to receive. A current values explanation or illustration can help show those changes under the policy's terms. Tax questions need qualified advice based on your situation, especially if coverage could end with debt outstanding. This article doesn't determine the tax treatment of a loan, withdrawal, or surrender.

## Keep the immediate need and the original purpose in view

Suppose, in a hypothetical situation, that you're considering a policy loan for an urgent home repair. The repair needs attention, and the loan may look attractive because the value is already associated with a policy you own. At the same time, that policy may be the resource your family expects to use for final expenses. Both needs belong in the decision.

Start with the actual amount you need and the insurer's explanation of what borrowing it would do. Then look at the funds intended for final expenses after the loan, including any other savings or coverage truly reserved for that purpose. That lets you see the tradeoff without assuming that borrowing is always a good or bad choice. The right decision depends on your needs, alternatives, and actual terms.

If you proceed, keep a dated record of the cash received, balance, rate information, and repayments. Review it against the next statement so a growing balance doesn't go unnoticed. The person helping with your final expense plan should know where the updated record is kept. You don't have to share every household financial detail, but they shouldn't be left planning around an original benefit amount that no longer reflects the debt.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can I borrow the entire death benefit?**

The death benefit isn't the borrowing limit. The amount available depends on the contract's loan provisions, current values, and existing debt. Get the current available figure rather than estimating it from the face amount or premiums paid.

**Does my usual premium payment also pay loan interest?**

It may not. Premiums and loan payments serve different purposes, and the insurer's payment instructions determine how money is credited. Confirm how to make the payment you intend instead of assuming the bank draft has changed.

**If I only borrow once, can the balance still change?**

Yes. Interest can add to the balance, while repayments can reduce it. A one-time loan therefore needs ongoing attention even if you don't take out another loan and the regular premium stays the same.
