**Quick answer:** A final expense life policy may allow a loan if it has enough loan value and its terms permit borrowing. A loan creates interest, and debt left unpaid can reduce what the people you name receive. A growing balance can also put coverage at risk. Before borrowing, learn what you can borrow and how it affects your final expense plan.

## A policy loan uses cash value as security

Some permanent life policies allow loans against cash value, which is value built up within the contract. The right to borrow depends on the contract and the value it has built up, rather than the final expense insurance label.

[Guardian's guide to life insurance loans](https://www.guardianlife.com/life-insurance/loans) explains how this borrowing differs from simply collecting part of a death benefit early. The loan is an obligation connected to the policy. Keep four figures on separate lines: the face amount, cash value, loan value, and debt. These can differ, even on the same statement. A large death benefit does not mean you can borrow that much.

## There may be little or no loan value early on

Not all of a premium becomes money you can borrow. The contract's values and loan terms set what you can request. A new policy may not yet have enough value for a loan because building that value takes time.

[Guardian's discussion of when borrowing may be available](https://www.guardianlife.com/life-insurance/withdraw/how-soon) emphasizes the role of cash value. Use a current figure from the insurer rather than estimating it from the number of premiums you paid. An illustration may show possible future values, which differ from what is available today. This matters if you are counting on a loan for a bill due now.

## Interest can change the balance without another withdrawal

A policy loan carries interest under the contract's terms. Unpaid interest may be added to the debt, so the balance can grow without you receiving more cash. So a one-time loan needs to be checked over time.

[Protective's policy loan explanation](https://www.protective.com/resources-for-you/learn-by-topic/life-insurance/basics/borrowing-money-from-life-insurance) describes how unpaid interest can increase the loan balance and affect proceeds. Note the rate, when interest is charged, and how repayments are credited. Paying the regular premium may not repay the loan. Premiums and loan payments serve different purposes, and a bank draft may cover only one of them.

## See how debt can change the amount paid

Here is a hypothetical math example, rather than a quote or a prediction about a policy. Assume a policy would otherwise pay $15,000 for a covered death. At that time, there is a $1,000 loan balance and $50 of unpaid interest. If only those amounts are taken out, the net benefit is $13,950.

The calculation is $15,000 minus $1,000 minus $50. It shows why the face amount may not match the funds the named person receives. The real result depends on the contract, debt, interest, and claim facts. Note the current expected net benefit in your plan and compare it with the amount you hope to set aside for final expenses.

## Coverage can be at risk if debt grows too large

The effect of a loan can extend beyond a smaller death benefit. Debt and interest can grow too large for the policy value to support, putting the contract at risk of lapsing, or ending. The exact math and notice rules depend on the contract and state law.

A loan still needs care even without a set monthly repayment bill. Read loan notices and policy-status notices as well as premium bills. A payment may keep the premium current without fixing a separate problem caused by debt. Ask what amount, if any, is needed to keep the contract in force. Check the deadline. Do not assume the coverage is secure.

## Borrowing, withdrawing, and surrendering are different actions

A policy loan creates debt. A withdrawal removes value under a provision that permits it. Surrender ends the policy in exchange for any net surrender value that is payable. These actions can have different effects and forms, so do not use “take money out” as the complete instruction when contacting the insurer.

Some contracts may also have an automatic premium loan provision. If elected and available, it may borrow to cover a missed premium. Its terms control how it works. That does not make the premium free or eliminate the resulting debt. A current values report or illustration can help show what a change would do. Tax questions need advice from a qualified professional. This article does not decide a loan's tax treatment.

## Keep the loan connected to the policy’s original purpose

Before deciding, write down the amount you need now and the final expense goal the policy serves. Note how the loan would change the benefit. Compare it with other funds truly set aside for that goal. You can then see the tradeoff. The right choice depends on your needs and the actual terms.

If you proceed, keep a dated loan record showing the cash received, balance, rate, and payments. Add the insurer's current benefit estimate. Review it when the next statement arrives. Tell the person helping with your plan where the updated record is kept. A folder showing only the old benefit amount can lead your family to expect more than the current net proceeds.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can I borrow the entire death benefit?**

The death benefit is not the loan limit. Borrowing depends on the contract and available loan value, after any relevant limits or existing debt. Obtain the current available amount rather than calculating it from the face amount.

**Does paying premiums repay a policy loan?**

Do not assume so. Premiums maintain coverage under the contract, while loan payments address debt and interest. Confirm how a payment will be applied before sending it.

**Can a small loan affect the benefit years later?**

Yes. Interest and later payments can change the balance over time. The relevant figure for a future claim is the debt then outstanding, not just the original cash received.
