**Quick answer:** Start with the pages listing the insurer, insured person, policy owner, coverage amount, and payments. Then read the benefits and the terms that limit or change them, including any riders. A summary points you to key facts. The full policy explains how the coverage works and which rules apply.

## Begin with the policy you actually received

A brochure describes a product. A quote describes a possible price. The policy delivered by the insurer states the contract terms for your coverage. Put that document at the center of your review, even if the sales material is easier to read.

Check that you have the complete set of pages. Include the policy schedule, any attached riders, and later notices that change the contract. A cover sheet or account dashboard can be useful, but it may not contain the full terms.

The [South Carolina guide to reading a policy](https://doi.sc.gov/957/Understanding-Your-Insurance-Policy) explains why the entire contract matters. It describes declarations, the insurer's coverage promises, exclusions, conditions, definitions, and riders. Life policies may arrange or label these parts differently. Use the table of contents and page references to find them. Insurers do not all use the same layout.

## Check the people and the company first

Find the full legal insurer name and policy number. These details help the company find your contract when you call. An agency or broker name may also appear in the packet, but that is not necessarily the business issuing the insurance.

Then check the insured person, the owner, and the beneficiary details shown. The insured person is the life the policy covers. The owner holds the policy. The beneficiary is the person or group named to receive the death benefit under the policy's terms. Those roles can belong to different people.

For example, someone may help a parent make payments while the parent remains the owner. Paying the bill alone does not tell you who can request a policy change. If the roles shown differ from what you meant to set up, contact the insurer to resolve that. Our [insurance records guide](/resources/organize-final-expense-insurance-records) can help you keep the correct names and contact details together.

## Read the amount beside the right label

The first pages may use labels such as face amount, specified amount, or death benefit. Read the definition that applies in your contract. A large number at the top of a page does not explain every benefit or every case in which the insurer would pay.

Look at the premium separately. That is the payment for the policy, not the amount of insurance. Check how often you pay, when payments are due, and any schedule of future payments. If the policy lists more than one figure, find which is the regular premium and what any other amount represents.

The [Texas life insurance guide](https://www.tdi.texas.gov/pubs/consumer/cb018.html) discusses payments, death benefits, and added riders separately. Use that same care when reading your pages. Your contract's own definitions explain its wording. Do not add figures from different sections together unless the policy says they belong together.

## Follow the terms beyond the summary page

A summary can point you to the main facts, but the benefit provisions explain more. Read the section about what the insurer agrees to provide. Follow page references to definitions, limits, exclusions, and other terms that affect that promise.

Definitions help when a familiar word has a precise meaning in the policy. Read the definition before filling in the meaning from everyday speech. If a provision sends you to another section, go there before deciding what the first passage says.

Conditions describe what you must do under the contract. These may address payments, changes, or the steps involved in a claim. Read them in the context of your policy rather than borrowing a rule from someone else's. Our guide to [life insurance brochure questions](/resources/life-insurance-brochure-questions) helps connect the short descriptions used while shopping with the fuller terms you receive after purchase.

## Include the riders and later changes

A rider adds to or changes part of the policy. It may have its own definitions, limits, payment details, or conditions. The base policy alone may therefore leave out an important part of your coverage.

Match the riders listed on the schedule to the attachments in the packet. Read what each one changes and what stays subject to the base policy. A heading that sounds helpful does not explain the feature on its own. Its terms are the useful part.

Keep later contract changes with the original pages. If you read an old copy while a newer rider applies, you could miss the current terms. Similarly, a past quote for an optional feature doesn't show that it was included in the issued policy. Verify what is actually listed and attached rather than assuming every feature discussed during shopping became part of your coverage.

## Resolve differences while the details are fresh

After reading, compare the delivered policy with what you intended to buy. Focus on names, coverage amount, payments, policy type, and attached features. If something differs, find the page and point to what needs to be cleared up. It is easier to resolve one clear question than a broad concern that the packet looks wrong.

The [Texas life insurance guide](https://www.tdi.texas.gov/pubs/consumer/cb018.html) urges buyers to review coverage and the information an agent provides. If you are unsure whether you have an active policy or which document is current, ask the insurer directly. A payment receipt alone does not answer every coverage question.

Also review the cancellation or review details supplied with your policy. Any deadline or right depends on the policy and applicable rules; don't borrow a number of days from an unrelated website. Keep the complete policy where it can be found, and let the person who may need it know how to reach the insurer.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What if my policy does not have a page called declarations?**

Insurers can use different labels, such as a policy schedule or policy data page. Look for the pages listing the insured person, policy number, coverage amount, and payment details. Use the table of contents or ask the insurer where these details appear.

**Can I use the brochure if I have lost the policy?**

A brochure can help identify the product, but it is not a replacement for your contract. Contact the insurer or agent to request the current policy documents. Use the policy number if available so they can find the correct coverage.

**Does the person who pays the premium automatically own the policy?**

No. The payer and owner can be different people. Read the ownership details in the policy and the insurer's records. If you need to make a change, first confirm who has that authority under the contract.
