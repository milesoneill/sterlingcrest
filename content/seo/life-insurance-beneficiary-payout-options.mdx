**Quick answer:** A life insurance death benefit is commonly paid as a lump sum, while some policies offer installments or other settlement methods. The available choices depend on the contract, insurer, and any valid selection already in place. Before choosing, connect the method with the bills and income needs ahead of you, then understand how much access you would keep to the remaining money.

## First separate the claim from the payout method

A beneficiary is the person or organization named to receive a life insurance death benefit. The death benefit is the amount payable after a covered death under the policy and the insurer's claim review. A settlement option concerns how those proceeds are delivered or held. Choosing a payment method is a different step from establishing what the policy owes.

If you are filing a claim after a loss, the choices may arrive at a time when you are already handling unfamiliar bills and decisions. Understanding the basic terms can make the paperwork easier to read. The [NAIC's beneficiary guide](https://content.naic.org/article/consumer-insight-what-should-i-know-about-life-insurance-beneficiaries) describes options that may be offered, including a single check and a retained asset account. It also stresses knowing the insurer, policy number, and benefit amount.

Begin with the options for the actual claim rather than assume every method described online is available. A valid selection made under the policy can also affect what the beneficiary may choose. The general concepts are useful background; the contract and claim instructions establish the real choices.

## A lump sum gives you access to the proceeds together

With a lump-sum settlement, the payable benefit is provided as one amount rather than a series of scheduled payments. That can be helpful when several expenses arrive close together, such as funeral costs and household bills. It also places responsibility for deciding how to hold and use the money with the beneficiary.

Receiving the full amount does not mean spending it all at once. The payout method and the spending plan are separate decisions. You might need part of the money soon while wanting more time to consider the rest. Access can give you that flexibility, but it does not supply a plan for the funds by itself.

Confirm how the insurer would deliver the proceeds and what remains to complete the claim. A check, an electronic payment where offered, and an insurer-held account are different arrangements. Selecting a method does not, by itself, guarantee an arrival date, so it is sensible to connect any payment commitments with the actual claim status rather than the name of the option.

## Installments organize payments over time

Some settlement methods spread proceeds across a schedule. A fixed-period arrangement, for example, pays over a selected period. Other methods may use a chosen payment amount or a different basis. The [Texas Department of Insurance guide](https://www.tdi.texas.gov/pubs/consumer/cb018.html) describes several general settlement choices, including fixed-period and interest options.

The attraction of a schedule is that it can fit recurring needs. The tradeoff is access to the rest of the money. Funds committed to scheduled payments may not be available in the same way as a lump sum, so it matters whether you can withdraw more, change the election, or end the arrangement. Those are contract questions to settle before relying on the money for an unexpected expense.

Also find out what sets the payment amount, when payments start, and what happens if the recipient dies. A fixed period and a lifetime arrangement do not promise the same pattern of payments. Similar-looking monthly figures can therefore describe different commitments, even before interest terms or any remaining amount are considered.

## An interest option is different from spending down the benefit

Under an interest-only arrangement, the insurer holds proceeds and pays interest according to the terms. That is different from an installment plan that distributes the proceeds themselves over time. The distinction matters if you expect each payment to reduce the original balance or want access to that balance later.

The [NAIC's guide to settlement methods and retained asset accounts](https://content.naic.org/article/consumer-insight-retained-asset-accounts-and-life-insurance) describes these general alternatives. For an actual interest option, understand whether the rate is fixed or variable, how it is credited, and what rights apply to the underlying proceeds. A quoted interest payment is not enough to explain the whole arrangement.

The practical question is what each payment represents. Money arriving from interest alone is doing a different job from money that steadily draws down the original balance. Understanding that difference helps you discuss the years of support you want and whether the arrangement preserves the access you expect.

## A retained asset account holds funds for withdrawal

A retained asset account, often shortened to RAA, generally holds insurance proceeds with the insurer and gives the beneficiary a way to draw against them. The NAIC describes it as a way of holding funds while considering further decisions. The papers may resemble a checkbook without creating an ordinary bank checking account.

Before using one, read how you can access some or all of the balance, how interest is credited, whether fees apply, and who holds the funds. Its own disclosures should explain the relevant protections and services. Familiar-looking paperwork is not a reliable basis for assuming that all bank-account features apply.

An RAA is also different from committing to fixed installments. Both may appear among settlement choices, but a place to hold funds for withdrawal is not the same as a contract to receive a payment schedule. Being clear about that difference can prevent a convenient-sounding label from standing in for an understanding of what you have selected.

Here is how the methods discussed so far differ at a glance:

| Method | How the proceeds are handled | Access question to resolve |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lump sum | The payable benefit is provided together | How would it be delivered, and what remains in the claim process? |
| Installments | Payments follow the selected schedule | Can you withdraw more or change the election? |
| Interest option | The insurer holds proceeds and pays interest under the terms | What rights apply to the underlying balance? |
| Retained asset account | Funds are held for withdrawal through the account arrangement | Who holds the funds, and what access, fees, and protections apply? |

## The insurer's instructions turn the general choices into a real decision

For an example of why the actual materials matter, MetLife's [life claim FAQ](https://www.metlife.com/support-and-manage/file-a-claim/life-claims/faqs/) describes a mailed check or its insurer-held account for the claims covered there. That is one insurer's published process, rather than a menu promised by every insurer or offered through Sterling Crest.

Read the instructions for your claim and ask the insurer to explain any unclear selection through a verified contact channel. You should be able to describe the result in ordinary words: the funds are being sent to you, held for withdrawal, or committed to scheduled payments. If that remains unclear, another explanation is more useful than making an election based on which name sounds familiar.

Before choosing, establish whether the election can be changed and what happens to any unpaid amount if the recipient dies. Tax, legal, trust, and estate questions need advice specific to those issues. The insurer can explain its offered terms; a qualified professional can address the separate personal questions those terms raise.

## Match the access with the time frames in your life

Consider, as a planning example, a beneficiary with a funeral invoice due soon and household expenses that will continue for years. Those are two time frames. A regular-payment option may sound appealing for monthly support, but the person still needs to know how the immediate bill would be paid. A lump sum offers different access, while leaving the longer-term spending decisions to the recipient.

List confirmed near-term bills separately from estimates and ongoing needs. Then compare those needs with the timing, access, and limits of the options actually offered. The goal is not to find one method that is universally best. It is to understand which tradeoffs matter for your situation before committing to them.

For policyholders planning ahead, current beneficiary details and accessible policy records are a useful contribution. Tell the appropriate person how to find the insurer and policy number. Those records do not decide a future settlement choice, but they give the beneficiary a clearer place to start when the time comes.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Must a beneficiary receive monthly payments?**

No universal rule requires that. A lump sum is common, while other choices depend on the insurer, contract, and any valid earlier selection. Confirm the available methods for the actual claim.

**Is a retained asset account the same as a bank account?**

Do not assume the same terms or protections. Read the account disclosures about who holds the funds, access, interest, fees, and services before deciding how to use it.

**Can I change a payout choice later?**

That depends on the arrangement. Establish the terms for changing an election, withdrawing funds, or ending payments before selecting it, particularly if future access to the money matters to you.
