**Quick answer:** Life insurance benefits are commonly paid as a lump sum. Some policies offer other ways to receive the money. The choices depend on the contract, insurer, and any valid selection already in place. Before choosing, find out when funds are available, how the payments work, and whether the choice can be changed later.

## A payout choice concerns how the money is received

The beneficiary is the person or organization named to receive a death benefit. The benefit is the amount payable under the policy after a covered death and claim review. A settlement option sets how those proceeds are delivered or held. It does not, by itself, settle whether a claim is payable.

The [NAIC's beneficiary guide](https://content.naic.org/article/consumer-insight-what-should-i-know-about-life-insurance-beneficiaries) describes choices that may be offered. They include a single check and a retained asset account. It also stresses knowing the insurer, policy number, and benefit amount. Those facts give a beneficiary a place to start.

Begin with the choices offered for the actual claim. A general list does not mean every insurer offers each one. It also does not mean a beneficiary can override an existing policy selection. Check that point before planning around a preferred method.

## A lump sum provides the proceeds together

A lump-sum settlement provides the payable benefit as one amount. It does not spread it across a series of scheduled payments. This can give access for bills that arrive close together, such as funeral and household costs. The beneficiary then decides how to hold and use the money.

Receiving the full amount does not require spending it at once. The payout method and the spending plan are separate choices. A person may want access to the money while taking more time to decide what to do with the part not needed soon.

Confirm the delivery method for the claim. A check and an insurer-held account are not the same process. Selecting a method does not guarantee a certain arrival date. The insurer must also complete its claim review and payment process. Avoid making a spending promise based only on the option's name.

## Installments trade immediate access for a schedule

Some settlement options spread payments over time. A fixed-period option, for example, pays over a selected period. Other plans may use a different basis. The [Texas Department of Insurance guide](https://www.tdi.texas.gov/pubs/consumer/cb018.html) describes several general choices, including fixed-period and interest options. The contract determines which choices apply.

The main tradeoff is access. A schedule may help organize recurring needs, but it may limit access to the rest of the money. Find what can be withdrawn, changed, or ended before relying on it for an unexpected bill. Do not assume scheduled payments work like a balance in a bank account.

Check when payments begin and what sets their amount. Also check what happens if the person receiving them dies. Plans may handle unpaid amounts in different ways. A shared label does not make the duration or terms the same.

## A retained asset account is a different choice

A retained asset account, or RAA, holds proceeds with the insurer and provides a way to draw against them. The [NAIC's retained asset account guide](https://content.naic.org/article/consumer-insight-retained-asset-accounts-and-life-insurance) describes this way of holding funds while considering other decisions. The papers may look like a checkbook. That does not make it an ordinary bank checking account.

Read how you can access all or part of the balance. Check how interest is credited, whether fees can apply, and how the account is protected. Find who holds the money. Use the account's own disclosures rather than assuming the features of another kind of account.

An RAA is also different from an agreement to receive fixed installments. Both may appear among claim choices, but they do different jobs. Make sure you know which one is being offered before comparing access or convenience. Do not choose from the familiar look of the paperwork alone.

## The insurer's claim instructions show the real choices

MetLife's [life claim FAQ](https://www.metlife.com/support-and-manage/file-a-claim/life-claims/faqs/) describes a mailed check or its insurer-held account for the claims covered there. This is one insurer's published process. It is not a promise of choices from every insurer or through Sterling Crest.

The example shows why a general list cannot replace claim instructions. The claim form and its materials explain what can be selected. If the terms are unclear, contact the insurer through a verified channel. Ask before making an election you do not understand.

You should be able to describe the choice in plain words. Is the money being sent to you? Is it being held for withdrawal? Or is it committed to a payment schedule? If those points remain unclear, the choice needs another explanation before it is made.

## Match access to the bills you expect

List expenses expected soon and needs that may continue later. Keep estimates separate from confirmed bills. Then compare those needs with the timing and access in each offered method. The goal is to see whether the method does what you need it to do.

For example, someone may face a funeral bill soon and monthly household costs later. Those are two time frames. A schedule that seems helpful for monthly income needs a closer look at how the immediate bill would be paid. This is a planning example, not a recommendation for a method.

Your priorities may include access, steady payments, or time to think. The terms determine whether a choice provides them. Before selecting, check whether the election can be changed. If tax, legal, trust, or estate questions matter, get advice for those issues. This article offers no annuity recommendation or tax strategy.

For those planning ahead, keep beneficiary details current and tell the appropriate person how to locate the policy. Clear records make a future claim easier to begin. They do not replace claim review or decide which settlement method will fit when the time comes.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Must a beneficiary take monthly payments?**

Do not assume so. A lump sum is common. Other choices depend on the insurer and contract. Check the choices for the claim and whether an earlier selection limits them.

**Is a retained asset account the same as a bank account?**

Do not assume the same terms or protections. Read its disclosures about access, interest, fees, and who holds the funds before deciding how to use it.

**Can a payout choice be changed later?**

That depends on the arrangement. Check the terms for changing an election, withdrawing funds, or ending payments before selecting it, especially if future access matters to you.
