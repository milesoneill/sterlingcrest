**Quick answer:** Start with the insurer's exact legal name, then use the NAIC's Consumer Insurance Search to review reported complaint details for the type of insurance involved and report years. Read the report's labels and notes. Complaint history is useful context, but it does not predict how your own policy or claim will be handled.

## Search the company that would issue the policy

A familiar brand name can cover more than one legal insurance company. That is why the first step in complaint research is finding the full insurer name on the proposal or policy. A broker's name, an agency's logo, and the issuing insurer's name can be different.

Match the full legal name carefully before drawing a conclusion from a result. Use the name on the policy or proposal, rather than relying only on a logo or brand. If several results appear, check which one actually relates to the policy you are looking at.

Suppose two companies share a similar brand. A complaint report for one may not describe the other. You can ask which legal entity will issue the coverage without making a purchase. Our [guide to insurance brokers and insurance companies](/resources/insurance-broker-vs-insurance-company) explains those roles if the names on your materials are confusing.

## Use the relevant type of insurance and time period

The [Texas insurance department's complaint resources](https://www.tdi.texas.gov/CONSUMER/complaint-data.html) link to state data and the NAIC's Consumer Insurance Search. The state describes a dataset with confirmed complaints and policies in force by company and line of coverage. The search tool provides company profiles, complaint information, financial details, and licensing reports.

For life coverage, read the life insurance details. A company may sell several kinds of insurance, and a report for another line answers a different question. Keep the reporting period visible as well. Data for one year should not be mistaken for a summary of all the years the company has been in business.

Compare the same type and year for both insurers where you can. Numbers from different reports may look alike while describing different things. You don't need a spreadsheet to do this well. A short note of the insurer name, report year, and category is enough to keep the search clear.

## A complaint count needs context

The raw number of complaints is only a starting point. A large insurer serves a different volume of business from a small insurer. Looking at count alone can leave out that scale. It can also hide whether a change reflects one year or a longer pattern.

A complaint index compares a company's share of complaints with its share of the insurance business being measured. For example, the [Indiana insurance department](https://secure.in.gov/idoi/consumer-services/complaint-index/) explains that 1.00 in its table means those shares are equal. A figure of 2.00 means the company's complaint share is twice its share of business. It does not mean that twice as many of its claims were denied.

Read the notes for the actual report you use. The market, type of insurance, year, and kinds of complaints counted matter. An index is not a star rating or a percentage of claims that went wrong.

Because the index compares shares, both the complaint figures and the size of the business affect the comparison. A move in the index deserves a closer look. It does not tell its own complete story.

## Look for a question the report can help answer

Complaint research works better when it serves a real concern. Perhaps you want to understand whether a pattern involves billing, delays, or communication. Look at the categories and details shown, rather than guessing at the cause from a total.

A report cannot recreate each customer's experience. You may not have that person's policy, application, claim details, or the insurer's full response. Avoid turning a limited public record into a conclusion about what happened in a case you have not read.

The same applies to your own expectations. A low complaint count cannot promise smooth service. A past complaint also does not show that your future claim will have a problem. Use the details to decide what deserves more attention. A clear question about service or policy terms is more useful than a broad label such as good or bad.

## Keep complaints separate from other company checks

Complaint history, financial strength, licensing, and policy terms address different questions. One cannot stand in for all the others. A financial strength rating, for example, is about an insurer's financial position. It doesn't tell you whether a customer found the billing process easy.

The [Texas life insurance guide](https://www.tdi.texas.gov/pubs/consumer/cb018.html) recommends checking both a company's financial rating and its complaint history. Those checks give you different kinds of information.

For the policy itself, compare the amount of insurance, payment rules, and terms that apply to you. For the seller, verify the license that applies. Our guide to [checking an insurance agent before requesting a burial quote](/resources/verify-life-insurance-agent-before-burial-quote) covers that separate step. None of these checks requires treating an internet review as proof of a company's overall quality.

## Know where to go if the issue is your own

Researching past complaints is different from seeking help with a problem you already have. If a charge, notice, or claim decision is unclear, start with the insurer's response and the policy details involved. Keep dates and records of your contact so you can describe the issue clearly.

If you need to raise an insurance complaint, contact your state insurance department. That department can explain its process and the details it needs. Filing does not promise a particular outcome, but it gives you an official route for raising the concern.

If you cannot find an insurer in a search, don't fill the gap with an assumption. Check the name and contact your state department for help verifying the company. Missing search results and a clean complaint history are different things.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is an online star rating the same as an NAIC complaint report?**

No. Online reviews and regulator complaint records come from different processes. The Texas regulator, for example, describes datasets with confirmed complaints and coverage details. Read each report's notes rather than combining its numbers with online stars into one score.

**Should one complaint stop me from considering an insurer?**

One record alone cannot answer whether a policy fits your needs. Consider the reporting period, company scale, detail shown, and other checks. The coverage itself and payment terms still need their own review.

**What if I find two insurers with almost the same name?**

Use the exact legal name on the proposed policy and check the matching company record. Related companies may be separate insurers. If the issuing company is unclear, have that named before using either report to make a decision.
