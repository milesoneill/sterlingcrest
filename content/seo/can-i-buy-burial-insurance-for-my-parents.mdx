**Quick answer:** Yes, in many cases. You can arrange burial insurance for a parent who agrees and takes part in the application. You also need a valid family tie or financial stake. Decide who will own the policy, pay the bill and receive its benefit. The insurer must approve the application.

## Your parent needs to be part of the application

You can do the research, compare options, and offer to pay. Your parent still needs to know about the policy and take part in the application. Paying for it doesn't replace their consent.

You also need **insurable interest**: a family tie or financial stake that gives you a valid reason to insure someone's life. [Immediate family members generally have an insurable interest](https://content.naic.org/consumer/life-insurance.htm), so buying coverage for a parent is different from trying to insure a stranger. The insurer checks that your plan meets its rules.

The consent steps follow state law and the insurer's forms. For example, [Florida generally requires the person being insured to apply or consent in writing](https://www.flsenate.gov/Laws/Statutes/2026/627.404), with some exceptions. Being someone's adult child doesn't give you permission to sign their name.

If you haven't raised the subject yet, start with what your parent already has in place. They may have a policy or funeral arrangements you don't know about. Our guide to [talking with parents about burial insurance](/resources/talk-to-parents-about-burial-insurance) can help with that first conversation.

## Who owns the policy if you pay for it?

Paying for a policy and owning it are separate roles. Some insurers allow you to pay the premiums while your parent keeps ownership. You may also be able to own the policy yourself. Settle this before you apply, since the owner holds the rights set out in the policy.

There are four roles to keep straight:

- **Insured:** The parent whose life the policy covers.
- **Owner:** The person who holds the policy and the rights it gives them.
- **Payer:** The person paying the bill. A premium is the cost of the insurance.
- **Beneficiary:** The person or people named to receive the death benefit under the policy's terms.

Those roles don't have to belong to four different people. You might pay the bill and also be a beneficiary, for example. The application needs to match the setup you and your parent want, within the insurer's rules.

If siblings split the cost, decide who will pay the insurer each month and how each person will send their share. Agreeing to split the bill doesn't change who owns the policy or who is named as beneficiary.

## Choose the coverage amount around the expense you want to cover

Start with your parent's plans. A direct cremation and a burial with a viewing are different arrangements, so they need different budgets. For a starting point, here are figures from the [NFDA's 2023 price-list study](https://content.nfda.org/news/media-center/nfda-news-releases/id/8134/2023-nfda-general-price-list-study-shows-inflation-increasing-faster-than-the-cost-of-a-funeral):

| Type of funeral | 2023 national median cost |
| --- | ---: |
| Funeral with a viewing and burial | $8,300 |
| Funeral with a viewing and cremation | $6,280 |

These are 2023 survey figures, not current local quotes. Both include a viewing and funeral service. The cremation figure is not the price of a direct cremation without a viewing. The totals leave out cemetery interment, a grave marker, and outside charges such as flowers or an obituary.

Use those figures to start the conversation, then build a budget around the arrangements your parent wants. The [FTC's funeral pricing checklist](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/funeral-costs-pricing-checklist) helps you check the funeral home's fees, goods and services, and outside charges. Check which items a package already includes before adding them again.

Next, subtract money or existing insurance set aside for these costs. For example, a hypothetical $10,000 funeral budget minus $3,000 reserved for it leaves a $7,000 gap. This shows a gap in the funeral budget. It isn't a policy price or advice about what to buy. Don't count savings your parent needs for living expenses or intends to use for something else.

You can work through [how much final expense coverage to consider](/resources/how-much-final-expense-insurance-do-i-need) during a phone conversation. You don't need a funeral estimate or a completed worksheet before booking.

## What affects your parent's monthly premium?

The insurer is covering your parent's life, so their age and health matter even if you're the one paying. Tobacco use, the coverage amount, and the policy's features can also affect the [cost of life insurance](https://www.tdi.texas.gov/pubs/consumer/cb018.html). A price in an ad isn't a quote based on your parent's details.

When comparing payments, keep the coverage amount the same. A lower monthly price may simply buy less insurance. You'll also want to understand whether the payment can change and what benefits and limitations come with it.

Choose a payment the person paying the bill can maintain. If you're covering it, include it alongside your own regular expenses. If your parent is paying from a fixed income, their budget should guide the discussion. The payment needs to work month after month, alongside the other bills.

## Compare what you're buying, then apply together

Once you have a coverage goal and a comfortable payment range, compare the actual policies. Check when coverage starts, what it pays, and any limits or exclusions, along with the monthly cost. These details explain what the policy does for the price.

If you and your parent choose to apply, your parent provides the details and consent the insurer needs. That may include questions about their health and medications. The insurer then reviews the application and gives you its decision and the terms it can offer.

When the policy is issued, check the names, owner, beneficiaries, coverage amount, and payments against what you agreed to. A quote or submitted application isn't proof that coverage has started. If your parent already has a policy, review it before considering a replacement and don't cancel it just because you've applied elsewhere.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can I pay for a policy my parent already owns?**

If your parent already has coverage, contact that insurer about its payment options before buying another policy. Helping with an existing premium may be worth considering. Paying the bill doesn't, by itself, transfer ownership to you.

**Can I help if my parent lives in another state?**

Start with the state where your parent lives. That affects which policies and licensed professionals are available to help them. Sterling Crest currently serves final expense clients in Florida, Georgia, Texas, Michigan, North Carolina, Tennessee, South Carolina, and Ohio.

**What if my parent can't give consent?**

Explain the situation to the insurer before applying. A family relationship or power of attorney should not be treated as automatic permission to meet every consent or signature requirement. Legal guidance may be needed for that situation.
