**Quick answer:** A funeral budget needs more than the funeral home’s bill. Include burial or cremation, cemetery costs, and personal extras. The NFDA’s 2023 national study reported a median of $8,300 for an adult funeral with viewing and burial. That excludes cemetery interment, a grave marker, and cash advances such as flowers. Use that figure as a starting point. Then build a total for your own plans.

## Start with the kind of arrangement you want

The word funeral can describe very different plans. A viewing and burial is one kind of plan. Direct cremation with a family gathering later is another. Decide which plan you want to price before comparing totals.

The [NFDA's 2023 study](https://content.nfda.org/news/media-center/nfda-news-releases/id/8134/2023-nfda-general-price-list-study-shows-inflation-increasing-faster-than-the-cost-of-a-funeral) reported these national adult-funeral medians:

| Arrangement | Reported median |
| --- | ---: |
| Funeral with viewing and burial | $8,300 |
| Funeral with viewing and cremation | $6,280 |

These are 2023 survey figures, not current local quotes. The cremation figure includes a viewing and ceremony; it is not a direct-cremation price. Both leave out cemetery interment and markers. They also leave out cash advances, such as flowers or an obituary.

These figures show the size of a full-service budget. Do not copy it into every family's budget. Someone planning a simple cremation needs a price for that service, while someone planning burial needs to account for the cemetery as well.

## Put the main costs in four groups

A useful list shows what you want, what it costs, and who supplies it. The [FTC's funeral pricing checklist](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/funeral-costs-pricing-checklist) separates provider services, merchandise, and cemetery costs. Start with the categories below, then cross out items your plan does not use.

| Group | What to put on your list |
| --- | --- |
| Funeral home services | Basic services, transfer, body care, viewing or ceremony, vehicles |
| Merchandise | Casket or cremation container, urn, outer burial container if needed |
| Cemetery or memorial location | Space, opening and closing, marker and installation |
| Personal and outside costs | Flowers, notices, travel, gathering or meal |

Put each charge in one group only. If an urn is already in a cremation package, label it included. If the cemetery has not priced a marker, label it unknown. That keeps an incomplete budget from looking finished.

## Compare complete plans, not just headline prices

A small package price can be accurate and still cover only one part of your plan. For example, a funeral home's price may cover its services while the cemetery bills for the burial space and opening the grave. Ask for the total for the arrangement you described, including any items supplied by another business.

Use the same plan with each provider. A phone request could be: We are considering a viewing and burial. Please explain the funeral-home total, the casket included, and which cemetery charges we need to get separately. That is more useful than asking for your lowest funeral price without describing the service.

The [FTC's phone-shopping guidance](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/shopping-funeral-services-phone-or-online) says funeral homes must answer questions about prices by phone. You can compare options before visiting. When you settle the arrangements, check the itemized statement against what you selected.

## Use a working total without pretending it is a quote

Here is a simple budget example, using made-up figures. Suppose your funeral goal is $10,000. You have $2,000 in savings set aside only for that purpose. The amount still to plan for is $8,000.

That subtraction does not mean an $8,000 insurance policy is the right choice or available to you. It shows the gap between a goal and the funds set aside for it. To make a real choice, look at the costs and the funds your family can use. Then review the terms of any insurance offer.

Do not count the same money twice. Savings needed for rent or everyday bills may not be funds you want to assign to a funeral. If family members plan to help pay, note that amount. Keep it separate from funds already set aside until their plans are clear.

## Finish with the next two decisions

First, identify the largest open cost in your own plan. It might be the cemetery, the service type, or whether a package includes the casket. Resolve that question before spending time on small extras. A few real figures help more than a long page of guesses.

Second, decide how you want to discuss funding the remaining goal. Our [coverage-needs guide](/resources/how-much-final-expense-insurance-do-i-need) explains how funeral plans, available funds, and existing insurance fit into that conversation.

You can book a phone consultation with Sterling Crest before every price is final. Explain whether you want burial or cremation. Discuss which costs you want to plan for. The call can focus on burial-insurance options and the amount you want to explore. Discuss a payment you can keep up with, too. An estimate or completed checklist is not needed to book.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Should I use the national median as my final budget?**

Use it as context, not a provider quote. Your service choices and local charges determine your own total. Keep the year and exclusions with any published benchmark so you know what the number describes.

**What is the easiest expense to miss?**

Check the cemetery portion separately when burial is planned. A funeral-home total can leave you with more to price, including the space, opening and closing, and marker. Also check whether selected merchandise is already in a package.

**Do I need a finished cost list before discussing insurance?**

No. Start with the type of plan, the amount you hope to prepare for, and your monthly budget. The phone call can help you compare coverage options while you work on the funeral costs.
