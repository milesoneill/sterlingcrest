**Quick answer:** Mortgage protection for a single parent should consider both the home loan and the support a child would need after the parent's death. Life insurance can provide a death benefit under the contract, but buying a policy doesn't decide who will care for a child, who will own the home, or who may manage a child's money.

## Decide what staying in the home would mean

Start with existing coverage, available savings, and the living arrangement you hope to support. Then identify any gap. A full mortgage payoff may be one goal, but it isn't automatically the right measure of every single parent's need.

You may want your child to remain in the current home. That wish involves more than clearing the debt. Someone would need to provide day-to-day care, arrange payments, and manage the property. A person who might become a child's caregiver may live elsewhere or have different practical needs.

This doesn't mean you have to resolve every legal document before discussing life insurance. It means the insurance amount should serve a realistic family plan. Separate your preference about housing from assumptions about guardianship, inheritance, or access to accounts.

For example, your goal might be to help a caregiver maintain the home for several years while a child finishes school. Another family might plan for a move and need money for a transition instead. Each choice leads to different cost questions even when the mortgage balances match.

## Count more than principal and interest

If the home would be retained, include taxes, homeowners insurance, utilities, association charges if applicable, and maintenance. Childcare and transportation costs may also change when the person doing those tasks is no longer there.

The [CFPB's mortgage payment checklist](https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/documents/cfpb_checklist_monthly_mortgage_payment.pdf) helps separate principal, interest, and escrowed property costs. Clearing the loan doesn't eliminate every housing bill. If a plan relies on paying the mortgage monthly, use the full relevant payment rather than just its principal portion.

A useful worksheet has three columns: the cost, who normally pays or provides it, and how it might change after your death. Include unpaid work, such as school transportation, when replacing that work would require a new expense.

## Review resources before adding coverage

List existing individual life insurance, confirmed workplace benefits, and savings available for the same goals. Record policy end dates and beneficiaries. If workplace coverage is part of your plan, confirm what happens when you change jobs rather than assuming it stays with you.

Don't count support from another person unless it is dependable and appropriate to include. Likewise, a hoped-for home sale price isn't a guaranteed source of immediate funds. You can keep an uncertain figure out of the calculation until it is supported.

The [Texas insurance regulator's life insurance guide](https://www.tdi.texas.gov/pubs/consumer/cb018.html) explains that the amount needed relates to debts, lost income, and continuing expenses. Its state-specific requirements aren't universal rules, but those planning questions help keep a mortgage discussion connected to the family's broader needs.

## A hypothetical calculation for a transition period

Suppose a parent wants to plan for a $180,000 mortgage payoff and $30,000 of separate transition costs. The combined goal is $210,000. If $50,000 of existing life insurance and $20,000 of available savings are assigned to those same needs, the initial gap is $140,000.

That is a planning example, not a quote or recommendation. It excludes other goals the family might have, such as years of child support or education. It also assumes the resources are actually available and that using them for these purposes is appropriate.

If you choose a monthly housing-support goal instead, calculate that directly. Don't add a complete mortgage payoff and many years of the same mortgage payment unless you have a clear reason; that can count one obligation twice.

## Be careful about naming a minor directly

A young child's name on a beneficiary form doesn't by itself create a practical system for handling a large payment. The [Texas life insurance guide](https://www.tdi.texas.gov/pubs/consumer/cb018.html) discusses complications when a minor is named. The permitted arrangements and legal consequences depend on applicable law and the contract.

Don't solve the issue casually by naming a friend and assuming that person must use the money for your child. A beneficiary designation, an informal promise, a trust, and a guardianship arrangement are different things.

Our [guide to naming a minor as a beneficiary](/resources/minor-life-insurance-beneficiary) explains why receiving and managing the money need separate attention.

The right next step is to coordinate the insurer's available designation process with qualified legal advice for your family. That review should explain who receives the benefit, who can manage it, and what instructions are legally effective. A life insurance discussion cannot appoint a guardian or interpret a court order.

## Match the coverage period to the job

Think about when your child's housing needs may change and when the mortgage is scheduled to end. These dates may not match. A term that ends before the planned support period can leave a gap; a longer period may address another continuing need but carries its own cost.

Review existing policy deadlines before replacing anything. You may already own useful coverage that can remain part of the plan. Our [mortgage protection overview](/services/mortgage-protection) can help frame the purpose, while a [phone consultation about your own household](/book) can focus on the amount and time period you want to compare.

The premium must also fit today's budget. A policy that strains essential bills can be hard to maintain. Discuss available amounts and features rather than assuming the largest benefit is always the most suitable choice.

## Make the information findable

Once a plan is in place, keep the insurer's name and contact details somewhere the appropriate person can locate. Keep policy records distinct from legal documents about care or property. Update both through the correct process when family circumstances change.

If a parent has already died, the task is to locate existing policies and benefits, not buy coverage for that past death. The mortgage servicer can explain its own process; legal questions about the home or children belong with qualified counsel.
