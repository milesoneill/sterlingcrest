**Quick answer:** A mortgage protection letter after closing may be an ad, even if it names your lender or shows your loan amount. Call the lender or servicer using a number from your own paperwork. If it’s an insurance offer, verify the seller and insurer with your state insurance department before sharing information.

## Why a sales letter can look like it came from your lender

You may find a postcard shortly after buying a house. It might list your lender at the top and mention your loan amount, then ask you to call about protecting your home. Those details can look reassuring. The [Consumer Financial Protection Bureau’s guide to what happens after closing](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/owning-a-home/close/after-closing/) explains that some home and mortgage information becomes public once the transaction is recorded. Someone can use that information in an ad without being part of your loan.

A mortgage protection life insurance offer is a sales offer for a policy meant to help with a home loan if the insured person dies. The name on the postcard alone tells you little about the product. It might be an individual life policy that pays a person you choose, or it might be a loan-linked product that pays the lender. You need written terms to tell which it is.

## Verify the notice in this order

1. **Find the company that handles your loan.** The mortgage servicer is the company that sends your statements and processes payments. It can be different from the lender that made the loan. Use the number on a mortgage statement or closing paperwork that you already have. If the loan is new and you don’t have a statement, start with your known lender contact.
2. **Ask what the company actually sent.** Say you received a letter asking about mortgage protection and ask whether it came from the servicer, whether the company knows the sender and whether your account needs attention. If the company says it needs proof of property insurance, ask how to send it through its verified channel.
3. **Read the sender’s legal name.** Look for small print about whether the sender is affiliated with the lender. If you want to consider the offer, ask for the full name of the agency, the agent and the insurer that would issue the policy. A brand name or an ID printed on the postcard gives you too little to check.
4. **Check the license independently.** Find your regulator in the [NAIC directory of state insurance departments](https://content.naic.org/state-insurance-departments) and use the state’s lookup or call its consumer line. Ask how to check that the agent and insurance company can sell that type of coverage in your state.

If the offer asks you to scan a code, call right away or send back a completed card, use the independent checks first. Keep account credentials, Social Security numbers and payment information off an unverified reply form or call.

## A hypothetical example with two different notices

Suppose Ana closes on a $260,000 home loan. Two weeks later a postcard lists that exact amount and says a response is due Friday. She calls the lender using a number from her closing packet. The lender confirms the card was sent by someone else and says the loan account needs no action. Ana can set the card aside. The matching amount was a clue about the recorded loan, and the call answered whether the letter applied to her account.

Now suppose a letter asks for proof that the home still has property coverage. Ana calls the servicer at the number on her statement. The servicer confirms it sent the letter and gives her instructions for uploading proof. In that case, she follows those instructions through the verified channel. The same first step works even when a notice turns out to be real.

## What to ask if you’re interested in the insurance

Request a written policy illustration or summary, then ask which document has the binding terms. Find the benefit amount and the beneficiary, meaning the person or organization that receives a payable life insurance benefit. Ask if the benefit stays the same or falls as the loan balance goes down. Check how long coverage lasts and whether the premium, the amount you pay for insurance, can change. If it’s advertised as a monthly benefit, find out how many payments it could make and when they stop.

Then compare the offer with coverage you already have. You may have an individual policy that already leaves enough for your family to keep paying the loan. If you need more information, our [guide to mortgage protection beneficiaries](/resources/mortgage-protection-insurance-beneficiary) shows why the recipient matters. You can [book a brief phone consultation](/book) to review an offer once you’ve verified the sender. Bring a summary with your account number and address covered. The benefit, policy term and intended recipient are enough to compare the basic structure.

## If you already responded to the letter

Save the postcard and note the number you called and what you shared. Contact your bank promptly using its verified number if you gave someone payment information. If you think an insurance offer misrepresented who sent it, the state insurance department can explain how to file a complaint. If you bought a policy, contact the insurer using its verified details and ask for a copy of the contract and its cancellation terms.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How did the sender get my mortgage amount?**

Some details about a recorded home purchase or mortgage can become public. Seeing the correct lender or loan amount on an ad doesn’t establish who sent it.

**Can I ignore a mortgage protection postcard?**

Call your lender or servicer through a number you already trust if you’re unsure what the card means. Once you’ve confirmed it’s an optional insurance offer and nothing is due on your account, you can decide whether to pursue it.

**Does the phrase “state regulated” mean my state sent the letter?**

Insurance regulation and sponsorship are separate. Verify the company’s license with your state department and ask the sender to identify the insurer and agency.

**What if the letter gives me a response deadline?**

Use a known number to verify whether your servicer sent the notice and whether there is an account deadline. A date printed on an ad isn’t evidence of a loan requirement.
