**Quick answer:** Life insurance through work may end, change, or stay in place under a retiree plan when you retire. Check your employer's current plan terms to find out. Confirm the amount that would remain, its cost, the steps you must take, and the dates involved before counting on it for funeral or family expenses.

## The amount in your benefits portal may change

An employee benefits screen shows what you have under the plan today. It may not show what you will have after your final day of work. That difference matters if your family is counting on the death benefit to cover a funeral or help a surviving spouse.

The [Texas life insurance guide](https://www.tdi.texas.gov/pubs/consumer/cb018.html) says employer life coverage typically ends when you leave a job. Check your own plan to learn what that means for your benefits. A plan may let you keep some coverage on terms that differ from those for workers still on the job.

For example, knowing that you have basic life insurance and extra coverage through payroll doesn't tell you whether both continue. Find each part. One answer about the basic benefit should not stand in for a separate answer about coverage you elected yourself.

## Find the plan information that applies to you

Start with the current life insurance certificate or plan booklet and the details supplied for retirement. Look for sections on when coverage ends, who can keep retiree benefits, and options after leaving work. An old enrollment form can help identify the benefit, but it may not reflect today's terms. The [Texas retirement guide](https://tdi.texas.gov/tips/think-about-your-insurance-in-retirement.html) recommends asking your employer for a statement of what you can take with you, including life insurance.

Give the benefits office your planned retirement date and ask how the life benefit is treated on that date. The useful answer is specific: what continues, what ends, and what you must do. If the documents and a conversation seem to conflict, have the plan contact resolve the difference before you rely on either.

Keep the insurer's name in view too. Your benefits office and the life insurer may handle different parts of the plan. Knowing who answers plan questions and who handles the insurance makes follow-up easier. You need the current terms, rather than a coworker's memory of an earlier retirement.

## Continuing coverage can mean different things

A plan may describe an option to keep coverage after leaving work. Read the terms of that option before comparing it with a separate policy. Find out whether the insurance stays under a group arrangement or would become a different contract. The answer should come from the current plan documents.

A heading in a booklet does not tell you the price, amount, or length of coverage. It also doesn't show that the choice is available in your situation. Find the section that applies before treating it as an option. If forms or payment are required, confirm the actual deadline with the plan contact or insurer.

Compare an option to keep coverage on its own terms. A payment that came out of your paycheck may not describe what you would pay later. Likewise, a choice to continue an amount of insurance does not show that all the other features stay the same. Read the option as a new decision with its own details.

## Match the benefit to the job it still needs to do

Retirement changes income, spending, and the people who depend on you. The amount that made sense when you had a salary and a mortgage may no longer describe your family's needs. On the other hand, leaving work doesn't erase a surviving spouse's bills or funeral costs.

Separate those goals. Is the benefit meant to help with a funeral? Replace part of household income? Leave money for someone else? One policy may serve more than one purpose, so avoid assigning its full amount to each goal at once.

The [Texas life insurance guide](https://www.tdi.texas.gov/pubs/consumer/cb018.html) starts with the people who depend on you and the bills they would face. Review the resources you already have alongside those needs. Our guide to [reviewing existing life insurance before buying burial coverage](/resources/existing-life-insurance-before-burial-coverage) can help you account for what you already have before discussing another policy.

## Budget for retirement, not your last paycheck

Once you know what would continue, put its payment alongside your expected retirement expenses. Include any separate life policy you already pay for. A familiar payroll deduction can feel small until you see it among the bills you will pay directly.

Then look beyond the first payment. Do the plan records describe future changes to cost or coverage? If they do, use those details in your budget. If they don't answer the question, have that part explained rather than assuming today's figure lasts indefinitely.

There is no need to start with a new purchase. Understanding the benefit you have is the first useful result. You may find that the plan, another policy, or money set aside already addresses a cost. Comparing separate coverage can follow if your review shows an unmet need.

## Leave your family an accurate picture

When the retirement decision is settled, update the details your family has. A benefits statement from your working years can give a false impression if the amount later changes or ends. Use the current insurer, plan or policy number, contact details, and benefit amount instead.

Our [retirement final expense planning guide](/resources/final-expense-planning-retirement-checklist) covers the broader planning questions. For workplace life insurance, the key is to finish with a clear account of what remains after retirement. Share where those records are kept with the person who may need them.

If you discover that an expected benefit won't continue, you can then review your other resources and any separate coverage available to you. The decision starts with the actual gap, rather than with the size of the benefit you used to have.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does paying for extra life insurance through payroll mean it is my own policy?**

Not necessarily. Extra coverage can still be part of an employer's group plan. Check the certificate or enrollment materials to find the arrangement and what happens when employment ends. The way you pay doesn't settle that question.

**Can I rely on what a retired coworker received?**

Their experience can suggest useful questions, but your coverage may involve a different plan, election, retirement date, or group under the plan. Use the current records that apply to you rather than assuming their benefit carries over to your situation.

**What if I already retired and cannot find my life insurance information?**

Contact your former employer's benefits office and the insurer if you know its name. Ask for the current status of the benefit and any relevant records or notices. Don't assume that an old benefits statement proves coverage is active today.
