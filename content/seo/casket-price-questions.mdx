**Quick answer:** Casket prices can span a wide range. Northwood Funeral Home in West Palm Beach lists caskets from $1,488 to $15,597 on its March 16, 2026 price list. That is one provider’s range, not a national average. The casket is only one part of a funeral budget; cemetery and service charges need their own place in the total.

## What does a casket price tell you?

The price describes the item being sold, not the entire funeral. First, decide how much of your funeral budget you want to spend on the casket. Then compare models. A choice within that limit can be a good starting point. It need not match the most elaborate item in the room.

[Northwood’s published range](https://northwoodfh.com/services/) is a concrete example of how far apart prices can be. Use it as a reference point while asking your own provider for current choices within your spending limit. The first model offered is not the only price point.

Match each price to a model and a clear description. A broad merchandise range does not tell you what is in stock or which item fits your plan. You are choosing an item for your own arrangements, not trying to reach a national spending target.

## Compare descriptions, not just photographs

Caskets can be made from different materials and sold with different finishes and features. The [FTC's casket guidance](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/funeral-costs-pricing-checklist) explains that they are largely sold for visual appeal. It also cautions that no casket can preserve a body forever.

Start with the same few details for each option: the model, material, size, price, and any feature you care about. If a description includes a warranty, identify what the warranty actually covers. A product name does not promise every possible result.

There is no need to learn every technical term before shopping. A clear description should help you understand what changes between two choices. If the explanation is mostly a more impressive name or finish, decide whether that difference is worth the extra money to your family.

## Keep the outer burial container on a different line

A casket and an outer burial container are different items. The outer container is placed around the casket in the ground; it is not another name for the casket itself. Ask the cemetery what its own arrangement requires before treating the merchandise list as complete.

That distinction matters to the budget. Paying for a casket does not settle the price of a burial space, opening and closing the grave, or a marker. It also does not settle the funeral home's service charges.

Our [funeral expenses checklist](/resources/funeral-expenses-checklist) places those costs in separate groups. Use one line for each actual charge. If a package includes the casket, name the included item and note whether choosing a different one changes the package total.

## Check an included casket before comparing packages

Suppose one funeral package includes a casket and another lists it separately. You cannot compare the package totals fairly until you know what each total contains. A lower package figure may leave a merchandise purchase outside it, while another may already include an item you would choose.

Use a short comparison table with the package total, the exact casket included, and the cost of any change you want. Ask for the revised total rather than trying to adjust it from a showroom price. The included item and any allowance or substitution terms should be clear.

The [FTC's provider-comparison guidance](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/choosing-funeral-provider) emphasizes comparing the cost of all the items together as well as individual prices. Judge the full plan you want. Do not choose a funeral home on the casket price alone.

## Consider the container needed for cremation

A burial casket has one role. A rental casket for a viewing and a direct-cremation container have other roles. Match the purchase to the actual arrangement. A family choosing cremation without a viewing needs prices for that plan. It does not need to shop for a burial plan.

The [FTC's funeral types guide](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/types-funerals) explains that direct cremation is a plan without a viewing or visitation and that providers offering it must offer an alternative container. If you want a viewing before cremation, ask the provider to explain the rental and container options for that plan.

Do not assume a rental price includes everything used for the cremation itself. Find out which item is rented and which container is used afterward. Then check the services in the total. This keeps a merchandise choice tied to what your family actually plans to do.

## Set a spending limit before making the choice

You can honor someone without choosing the costliest model. Discuss preferences with the person helping make arrangements, including any religious or family wishes. Give the provider a spending limit and ask which current choices fit it.

Then look at the complete funeral goal. If spending more on the casket would mean using money reserved for another part of the plan, make that tradeoff clear before deciding. A firm limit can make the choice easier at a time when many decisions feel personal.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Do I have to choose one of the first models shown?**

Ask to see the price list and the options within your own spending limit. Display order does not tell you which choice fits your family. Compare descriptions and the full arrangement instead of treating the first few items as your only options.

**Does a more expensive casket preserve the body forever?**

No. The FTC cautions that no casket can do that. If a product has a warranty or named feature, read what it actually describes rather than assuming a broader result.
