**Quick answer:** The national median embalming charge was **$845 in the NFDA's 2023 price study**. That is a historical service benchmark, not a current local quote or a complete funeral budget. The same study's $8,300 funeral-with-viewing-and-burial total included embalming and excluded cemetery and outside charges. [Source: NFDA 2023 study](https://content.nfda.org/Portals/0/12-8-2023--2023%20GPL%20Survey.pdf).

## Why does a viewing change the conversation?

Embalming isn't required for every funeral. A public viewing may lead a funeral home to require it under its own policy, while a prompt direct burial or direct cremation can avoid it. Refrigeration may be an alternative, depending on timing, the arrangement, and applicable rules.

For final expense planning, preparation is one part of the total funeral budget. Compare the full arrangement with savings and existing life insurance before deciding whether another funding gap remains. Choosing or declining embalming doesn't, by itself, establish how much burial insurance a family needs.

Embalming is a preparation procedure used in some funeral arrangements. It is separate from other preparation such as dressing, grooming, or placing the person in a casket. If you want an open-casket public viewing, the provider may explain that embalming is a condition of offering that service.

That business policy shouldn't be presented as a law requiring embalming after every death. The [FTC Funeral Rule guidance](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/ftc-funeral-rule) distinguishes routine legal requirements from funeral-home policies and practical preparation needs. It also suggests asking whether a private family viewing without embalming is available.

This doesn't mean every provider must offer every kind of viewing. Explain the gathering you want, the expected timing, and whether the body will be present. You can then compare an actual alternative rather than simply deleting a line from a package that was designed around a different service.

## Where does refrigeration fit?

Refrigeration is a method of holding the body while arrangements proceed. The FTC explains that some states require embalming or refrigeration after a particular time, while others don't require routine preservation. In most cases, refrigeration is an acceptable alternative; the precise arrangement and location still matter.

Ask how the provider charges for holding and care. A refrigeration charge may be quoted by a period of time, included in an arrangement for a limited period, or handled another way. Find out what happens if paperwork or cemetery availability extends the wait. There is no need to turn a local schedule into a supposed national rule.

If you are arranging transport to another location, the receiving provider and carrier may have their own requirements. Those requirements should be explained for the actual route. Don't assume embalming is always required for transport, or that refrigeration alone always meets every transport condition.

## Direct burial and direct cremation use a different service plan

The [FTC's funeral-types guide](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/types-funerals) explains that direct burial and direct cremation take place without a viewing or visitation beforehand. These simpler arrangements avoid embalming as part of that service plan. A family may still hold a memorial later.

Consider two hypothetical preferences. One family wants a public viewing followed by burial. Another wants a direct cremation and a gathering at home two weeks later. The preparation needs differ because the plans differ. The second family hasn't selected a lesser remembrance; it has chosen a different sequence of services.

When you compare prices, use the complete plans. A lower embalming charge doesn't tell you which total arrangement costs less. Our [funeral expenses checklist](/resources/funeral-expenses-checklist) helps separate preparation, facilities, transport, cemetery charges, and optional memorial items.

## What should the written charges explain?

The [FTC's funeral pricing checklist](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/funeral-costs-pricing-checklist) explains that embalming and other preparation can appear as separate services. It also describes permission and disclosure requirements under the Funeral Rule. A covered provider generally cannot perform and charge for embalming without permission unless applicable law requires it.

Request a clear explanation of each preparation charge and why it belongs in your chosen arrangement. If the provider says a service is required, ask whether that requirement comes from law, its own policy, a cemetery, or another party. The itemized statement should make required purchases understandable.

Use the historical benchmark to understand the scale of one service, then obtain a current itemized arrangement. Don't add embalming again if it is already included in a package. If refrigeration replaces it, compare the complete care plan, including how long the quoted charge covers. Simply subtracting an old national median from a current package doesn't establish your savings.

## What does this mean for burial insurance planning?

If you're planning ahead, write down the type of gathering you prefer and the preparation it would involve. Include the full provider estimate and separate cemetery or crematory costs, not just the embalming line. Then account for money already designated for those expenses and policies already in force.

A life insurance death benefit can help the named beneficiary manage expenses after a covered death, subject to the policy. It isn't a promise that a particular preparation procedure will be paid as a separate reimbursement. Nor does the existence of cash value mean the entire policy amount is available to spend now.

## What if you're arranging services now?

If the death has already occurred, use the existing policy, insurer's claim process, and currently available funds. Let the provider explain its payment deadline before choosing services. An insurance claim can have a different schedule from the preparation and burial arrangements, so don't treat an expected payout as cash already received.

New life insurance cannot fund a past death. That distinction keeps immediate arrangements focused on available options and keeps future planning from becoming an inappropriate sales conversation during a claim.
