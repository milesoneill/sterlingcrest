**Quick answer:** Read a life insurance brochure as a starting point for questions. Find the company and product it describes, note the date or version if shown, and mark terms you want explained. Keep it separate from a personal quote, an application, and an actual policy. A general brochure does not establish your own coverage or eligibility.

## Find the page you are reading

Start with the title and company name rather than the largest phrase on the page. Ask whether the material describes the actual option you are discussing. If you received it some time ago, ask whether the same material is still right. A familiar logo alone does not answer that question.

You may have a full brochure, a single page, or an image someone shared. Note which one it is. A cropped image can leave out the context needed to understand a phrase. Ask about the full material rather than drawing a conclusion from one highlighted line.

## Keep marketing and personal records separate

A brochure gives general details about what it describes. Your own discussion may also involve an application, a quote, or policy material. Do not use those labels as though they mean the same thing. Ask what each item is and which question it can answer.

The Texas regulator's [life insurance guide](https://www.tdi.texas.gov/pubs/consumer/cb018.html) offers consumer background on understanding insurance. It cannot establish what a brochure means for your individual situation. Ask the insurer or agent to connect an answer to the actual option being discussed, rather than assuming the page is a personal recommendation.

## Mark language you cannot explain in your own words

Circle a term or copy a short phrase into your own notes. Then ask, "What does this mean for the option we are discussing?" You do not need to know the right technical question before asking for help. Being able to repeat the wording is enough to begin.

If an answer introduces another unfamiliar phrase, ask for what that phrase means too. Avoid turning a quick summary into a conclusion about benefits, restrictions, or eligibility. Those questions require the right details. This guide helps you find them; it does not interpret an actual product for you.

## Use the same questions for a shared screenshot

Imagine that a relative sends you an image of an insurance advertisement. You like the general idea but cannot see the company name or the rest of the page. Save your question rather than assuming that the image describes an option available to you.

You could ask which company issued it, what product it refers to, and where the full explanation can be found. Keep the relative's opinion separate from the source of the details. A well-meant recommendation may help you start a conversation, but it does not replace your own questions or confirm any insurer decision.

## Follow an answer back to its source

When someone explains a phrase, ask which material they are using. Note the title or page if that helps you follow along. If the answer seems different from what you read, return to the exact wording and ask what you missed. A calm read-back can reveal that you are discussing two different items.

The New York regulator's [consumer guide](https://www.dfs.ny.gov/consumers/life_insurance) encourages consumers to understand insurance questions before deciding. Use general guides for background and actual product material for exact questions. Do not carry state-specific statements into another jurisdiction without proper confirmation.

## Use a brochure question checklist

Choose the prompts that help with the material in front of you. You do not need a brochure or a completed checklist to schedule a discussion.

- [ ] **Source.** Which company or organization issued this material?
- [ ] **Scope.** Which product or topic does it describe?
- [ ] **Version.** Is the material right to the option being discussed now?
- [ ] **Context.** Am I looking at the whole explanation or only an excerpt?
- [ ] **Meaning.** Which phrase would I like explained in everyday language?
- [ ] **Personal question.** Which point needs the details of my actual situation?

Keep any personal details out of a public comment or shared image. General questions can be asked without posting policy numbers or private application details.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does a brochure prove that coverage is in place?**

Do not treat general marketing material as proof of your own coverage. Ask the insurer about the actual record and right documents. A brochure, a personal discussion, and an issued policy are different records.

**What if my copy has no clear date?**

Ask the company or agent whether it is the material right to the option now being discussed. Keep that question open rather than guessing from the appearance of the page or the date you received it.

**Should I ignore small print?**

Read the full explanation and ask about wording you do not understand. A headline alone may not answer your question. This guide does not interpret an actual restriction or tell you which terms apply to an individual policy.
