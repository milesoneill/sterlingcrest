**Quick answer:** Naming a child who is under the age of majority as a life insurance beneficiary can complicate how the money is paid and managed. Ask the insurer what happens under its contract and your state’s rules. If the child and the person paying for the funeral have different needs, make a separate plan for each.

## Start with who would receive and manage the money

A beneficiary is the person or organization named to receive a life insurance death benefit. If the beneficiary is a minor, which generally means they are below the applicable age of majority, payment can involve extra steps. The insurer can explain how it handles a claim. State law and the policy can affect who is allowed to receive and manage the proceeds for the child. [NAIC Life Insurance Buyer’s Guide](https://content.naic.org/sites/default/files/publication-lig-lp-consumer-life.pdf)

Ask what the insurer would need if a claim were filed today. You may want to know who would contact the insurer, how a representative would show their authority, and what documents could delay a payment if they are missing.

## Ask about the arrangements your insurer can record

A custodian is someone legally authorized to manage property for a child under the relevant arrangement. A trust can provide instructions for managing property, and a trustee carries out those instructions. Both can require specific wording and documentation. Ask an estate attorney which arrangement, if any, fits your situation. Ask the insurer how the form records that arrangement.

| Question to ask | Who can answer it |
| --- | --- |
| What happens if the child is still a minor when there’s a claim? | Insurer, with state-law questions referred to an estate attorney |
| Does the company accept a custodian designation, and what wording does it require? | Insurer and estate attorney |
| If a trust is involved, how should it be identified on the form? | Insurer and the attorney who prepared or reviewed the trust |
| What happens if the intended person or arrangement is no longer available? | Insurer and estate attorney |

If you decide to change the designation, ask for written confirmation when the insurer accepts it. Save the confirmation with your policy and note which version is current. Keep the insurer’s contact information where the person handling your affairs can find it. [NAIC Life Insurance Buyer’s Guide](https://content.naic.org/sites/default/files/publication-lig-lp-consumer-life.pdf)

## Keep funeral costs separate from money for the child

Say you want to leave $20,000 for a child’s needs, and the family expects $7,000 in funeral expenses. Ask which money the person arranging the funeral can actually use. If the policy proceeds are intended for the child and subject to a legal arrangement, ask how the funeral would be paid separately.

Write two lines in your plan. One line can show the amount you want the child to receive and who would oversee it. The other can show the funeral estimate, any savings set aside, and who would deal with bills. Avoid assuming that a relative who takes care of the child will automatically be able to use the life insurance proceeds for a funeral. Confirm the arrangement with the insurer and a qualified estate attorney.

## Tell the right people where to find the documents

Give the adult who would handle a claim the insurer’s name and the location of the policy. If a trust or custodial arrangement is involved, ask your attorney which records the adult should be able to find and how they should be stored. Keep that information separate from the funeral arrangements if another family member will be handling those costs. Store copies safely rather than sharing sensitive documents in a group message.

## Revisit the designation after major changes

A child may reach adulthood, a proposed custodian may move, or a trust may be updated. Contact the insurer to confirm the current designation after one of those events. Ask whether you can name a backup, also called a contingent beneficiary, and when that designation would apply. Update the insurer’s records through its process and retain the confirmation.

If you are deciding whether another policy could fund funeral costs separately, [schedule a consultation](/book) and bring the two amounts. The insurer can explain its beneficiary form, and an attorney can advise on the legal arrangement for a minor.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can I just name the adult who is raising the child?**

Naming an adult personally and legally directing money for a child are different arrangements. Ask an estate attorney about the consequences and ask the insurer what wording the form accepts.

**Who receives the benefit if the child becomes an adult before I die?**

Ask the insurer to confirm its recorded designation and what documentation would be required. If a trust or custodial arrangement is in place, check its terms with your attorney.

**What happens if I’ve already named a minor?**

Ask the insurer what it has on file and what a claim would require. If you choose a different arrangement after getting advice, use the insurer’s process and request written confirmation.
