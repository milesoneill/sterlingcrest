**Quick answer:** Before paying for a funeral plan, ask what the written agreement includes, who is responsible for each part, and where to find answers about changes. Read the actual terms rather than relying on a verbal summary. Keep unresolved questions separate from confirmed details. Seek suitable professional guidance when a question requires a legal interpretation.

## Identify the agreement and the people involved

Note the provider’s name and the offer date. Add the title of the document you are reading. Ask which business or person can answer questions about each part. A conversation about future plans is not the same as understanding a written agreement. Check which document your notes describe. An offer is not the same as a brochure or a sample.

## Ask what is included and what remains open

List the services and items described in the agreement, with prices beside the items actually quoted. Ask how the money will be held and what happens to any interest. The FTC’s [advance funeral planning article](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/planning-your-own-funeral) raises these questions for consumers. Read the provider’s actual answers; do not assume every offer handles payment in the same way.

## Read the wording about changes

Ask what happens if you move, choose another provider, or want to cancel. Where does the agreement explain any refund, transfer, or extra charge? Also ask what happens if the business closes. These are questions raised by FTC planning guidance, not promises about your agreement. Seek suitable professional advice if the wording raises a legal question you cannot resolve.

## Keep pricing and contract questions together

Save the written cost breakdown with your question list. The FTC’s [funeral pricing guidance](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/funeral-costs-pricing-checklist) can help you identify the items being discussed. Avoid carrying a short package label forward as a full description. Note who supplied an answer and when, especially if the proposed plans have changed during your conversations.

## Walk through a change before you sign

One way to understand an offer is to ask about an example that could matter to you. Suppose you might move closer to an adult child. Ask where the agreement explains what would happen to the plan after a move. Which parts could change? Who would answer the question? Avoid assuming that a familiar package name settles the point.

You can use the same approach if you later want a different service. Ask the provider to show you the relevant wording and explain any amount that would remain open. The answer needs to fit the actual agreement. A general explanation from another provider or an old brochure may describe a different arrangement.

A simple note can make the conversation easier to follow: the change you asked about, the page discussed, and the answer received. If the wording remains unclear, pause before making a decision and seek the right professional guidance. You do not need to treat an unanswered question as an agreed term merely to finish the conversation.

## Sort questions into three useful groups

Keep the purpose of each question clear. This helps you return to the person who can answer it and avoids treating one business as the source for every part of your plan.

- [ ] **Services.** Which items are described, and which choices are still undecided?
- [ ] **Money.** How will funds be held, and what does the agreement say about interest?
- [ ] **Changes.** Where are cancellation, transfer, refund, and extra-charge terms explained?
- [ ] **Responsibility.** What happens if the business closes, and who can explain that wording?
- [ ] **Insurance.** Do I have separate questions about my family goals or an actual policy?

Keep the last group for an insurance discussion. Sterling Crest's phone consultation concerns insurance planning; it does not replace advice about the funeral contract. You can ask about your goals while you are still gathering information. There is no need to sign a funeral agreement or complete this checklist before scheduling that call.

## Give yourself a clear decision record

Before taking another step, summarize what you understand and what you still need to ask. Keep the funeral agreement separate from any insurance documents. The [final expense overview](/services/final-expense) offers a place to discuss insurance-planning questions. It does not give advice about your prepaid contract. No funding approach is right for every household.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does this checklist tell me whether to prepay?**

No. It helps you identify questions about a specific offer. Your decision may depend on the actual agreement and your circumstances.

**Can I assume unused items are refundable?**

Do not assume a refund rule. Find the relevant wording and get appropriate guidance if you need help interpreting it.

**What if someone summarizes a term differently from the document?**

Mark the difference and ask for clarification. Keep the written agreement as the reference for the question you need answered.

**Is a funeral agreement the same as an insurance policy?**

Keep the documents and questions separate. Do not infer insurance terms from a funeral-provider offer or the reverse.

**What if I feel rushed to make a decision?**

Return to the points you still do not understand and ask for an explanation of the actual agreement. Keep a clear list of open questions. If a term needs legal interpretation, seek suitable advice before relying on your own guess. A general planning guide cannot tell you that a particular offer is right for you.
