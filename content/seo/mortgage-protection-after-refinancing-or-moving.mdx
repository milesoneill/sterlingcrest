**Quick answer:** After a refinance or move, compare your new loan balance and payoff date with the amount and remaining years of your life coverage. You may still have useful protection, or the housing change may reveal a gap. Individual life insurance and loan-linked coverage work differently, so confirm the actual contract before assuming coverage transfers, ends, or needs replacing.

## A new mortgage can change the job you want insurance to do

Refinancing means replacing an existing mortgage with a new loan. As [Freddie Mac explains](https://myhome.freddiemac.com/refinancing/planning-to-refinance), the new loan has its own term, rate, and payment. You might refinance to change the monthly bill, shorten or extend the payoff period, or meet another financial goal. Moving can change the balance and the household's costs as well.

Life insurance bought with the old mortgage in mind does not automatically adjust to those changes. That does not mean it has become useless or that a new purchase is required. It means you have a new set of numbers to compare with the policy you already own.

Start with the outcome you want for your family: a full payoff, a smaller loan, or time to make housing decisions after your death. Then look at the new debt and the available benefit. This keeps the review tied to a family need instead of treating the closing itself as a reason to replace coverage.

## Find out whether the coverage is tied to you or to the old debt

An individual life policy insures a person's life, even when it was purchased for mortgage protection. The beneficiary is the person named to receive the death benefit, and the premium is the payment required for coverage. A property change by itself does not tell you that the insurer must issue a new policy. The existing contract, its ongoing requirements, and any assignment determine what continues.

Credit life linked to a lender serves a different purpose. The [OCC describes credit life coverage](https://www.helpwithmybank.gov/help-topics/personal-auto-loans/credit-life-insurance/credit-insurance-life.html) as associated with repayment of a covered loan. If that debt is paid off or replaced, you need to establish what the agreement says about ending coverage, transfers, and any refund provisions. Do not assume it automatically covers the new mortgage.

This is why a broad promise that mortgage protection “follows you” is not enough. You need to know which arrangement you have and what its terms allow. Homeowners insurance is another separate contract; updating it for a new address does not answer what happens to life coverage.

## Compare the balance and the timeline separately

A larger new loan can change how much of the debt an unchanged life benefit could cover. A longer loan can create a timing gap even if the amount barely changes. Looking only at the new monthly payment can miss both issues.

Term life insurance provides coverage for a stated period. Imagine a household with a term policy that has 18 years remaining. It refinances into a mortgage scheduled to run for 25 years. If the family intends to rely on that policy through the entire loan, the final seven years are outside its current term. This hypothetical example does not prove that a new policy is the answer. It shows the question that needs resolving.

The family might expect other resources to meet the later need, or its goal might be support during the years children are dependent rather than a payoff at any point. Another household may want protection aligned with the full mortgage period. Once you can describe the gap, you can weigh those possibilities without assuming that a lower payment means the coverage still fits.

## A smaller loan may change the plan without removing every need

A move to a lower-cost home or a large payment toward principal may reduce the amount you want protected. Before reducing or canceling insurance, consider whether the same benefit now serves another important purpose. Your family could still need income support, childcare, or money for a period away from work after a death.

Housing costs also extend beyond the loan. A different home may bring different property taxes, insurance costs, repairs, or association dues. Even a full payoff leaves those bills. Include them when you consider whether a survivor could afford to stay.

The reverse can be true too: a family may have fewer dependents, more savings, and a smaller debt than when the policy began. Reviewing coverage can confirm that less help is now needed. The useful question is what the policy contributes to your current plan. Its original mortgage purpose and its current value to the family do not have to be identical.

## A closing does not update every insurance record

Keep your address and payment details current with the insurer, especially when several bills and accounts are changing at once. Review the named beneficiary if the move accompanies a marriage, separation, or another family change. A correct mailing address and a current beneficiary are separate tasks.

If there is a collateral assignment, a creditor has rights in the policy under that agreement. [Progressive explains how this arrangement differs from a beneficiary designation](https://www.progressive.com/answers/life-insurance-collateral-assignment/). A payoff or refinance is a reason to establish whether the assignment record needs a release or other update. Do not assume that selling a property automatically removed it from the insurer's file.

For loan-linked coverage, a possible cancellation or refund provision is something to verify, rather than money to assume is owed. Locate the applicable agreement and have the relevant record confirmed. These details matter because a family may otherwise expect a benefit that is no longer available, or overlook a policy that remains useful.

## Consider replacement only after understanding what you have

If the review reveals a gap, a proposed new policy deserves its own comparison. Underwriting is the insurer's process for evaluating an applicant and deciding what it will offer. Your new price or terms may differ from the policy you bought years ago, and an application or estimate is not the same as coverage in force.

The [NAIC's buying guidance](https://content.naic.org/article/consumer-insight-want-purchase-life-insurance-here-are-tips-help-you-through-process) recommends studying a replacement carefully. Compare what you would gain, what you would give up, and how the change would take place before canceling existing protection. A new loan does not make the old policy's guarantees irrelevant.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Do I need new life insurance every time I refinance?**

A refinance is a reason to compare the new loan with your existing protection. It does not establish a need to replace it. The review may confirm that the policy still fits or identify an amount, timing, or loan-linked contract issue to address.

**Will mortgage protection automatically cover my next home?**

That depends on the arrangement. Individual life coverage may continue under its own terms, while loan-linked coverage can be specific to the old debt. Confirm the contract and any assignment before treating the new mortgage as covered.

**What if I have paid off the mortgage?**

Revisit the policy’s role in the family plan. Other needs may remain even when the loan is gone. Also resolve any loan-linked termination or refund question and any assignment record, rather than assuming the payoff settled all insurance details.
